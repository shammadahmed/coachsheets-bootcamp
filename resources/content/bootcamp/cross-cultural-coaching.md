# Lesson 6.1: Cross-Cultural Coaching — Cultural Competence in Practice

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- Define cultural intelligence (CQ) and explain its four capabilities
- Identify common cultural assumptions that can derail coaching relationships
- Apply a framework for working respectfully across cultural, racial, national, and religious contexts
- Distinguish between asking genuine questions and imposing one's own cultural lens
- Adapt coaching models and communication styles to honor diverse worldviews

---

## Why Cultural Competence Matters

Coaching originated primarily in Western, individualistic contexts. As the profession globalises and as coaches work with increasingly diverse clients domestically, cultural competence moves from "nice to have" to "essential skill." A coach who cannot recognise their own cultural assumptions risks imposing those assumptions on clients, eroding trust, and limiting the coaching's effectiveness.

The International Coaching Federation (ICF) includes cultural awareness in its Core Competencies. Competency 4 (Cultivates Trust and Safety) and Competency 5 (Maintains Presence) both require the coach to respect the client's identity, context, and worldview. This is not about learning every cultural custom — it is about developing the humility and curiosity to explore what matters to each client.

---

## Cultural Intelligence (CQ)

Cultural intelligence is the capability to relate and work effectively across cultures. Researchers Soon Ang and Linn Van Dyne identified four capabilities that form the CQ model:

**1. CQ Drive** — Your motivation and interest in adapting to cross-cultural situations. Without genuine curiosity, the other three capabilities cannot develop. This includes confidence in your ability to handle cross-cultural encounters and a willingness to persist through discomfort.

**2. CQ Knowledge** — Your understanding of how cultures differ. This includes knowledge of cultural norms, values, economic and legal systems, and how these shape behaviour. You do not need to be an expert on every culture, but you need to know what questions to ask.

**3. CQ Strategy** — Your ability to plan, interpret, and adjust in cross-cultural interactions. This means being mindful of what is happening in the moment, checking your interpretations against reality, and drawing on cultural knowledge appropriately.

**4. CQ Action** — Your behavioural flexibility. Can you adapt your verbal and non-verbal communication? Can you adjust your coaching approach — for example, shifting from direct challenge to gentle inquiry if the client's culture values indirect communication?

> "The single most important capability in cross-cultural coaching is the willingness to be wrong about your assumptions." — David Livermore, cultural intelligence researcher

## Common Cultural Dimensions That Affect Coaching

While every client is an individual, research has identified cultural dimensions that frequently arise in coaching relationships:

| Dimension | Individualist Orientation | Collectivist Orientation |
|---|---|---|
| Goal-setting | Client's personal goals | Goals involving family/community |
| Accountability | Client owns responsibility | Shared responsibility with group |
| Feedback | Direct, honest feedback preferred | Indirect, face-saving feedback preferred |
| Authority | Coach as facilitator | Coach as expert/elder |
| Time | Linear, future-oriented | Cyclical, present-oriented |

**Power distance** — In high power-distance cultures, the client may expect the coach to provide answers, advice, and direct guidance. The coach who strictly refuses this role may be seen as unhelpful. The culturally competent coach explores what the client's expectations are and negotiates the coaching relationship transparently.

**Communication context** — Low-context cultures (e.g., Germany, United States) rely on explicit verbal communication. High-context cultures (e.g., Japan, Saudi Arabia) rely on implicit cues, body language, and shared understanding. A coach from a low-context background may miss subtle signals, while a coach from a high-context background may feel something is "off" without being able to articulate it.

## A Framework for Cross-Cultural Coaching Conversations

When working with a client from a cultural background different from your own, consider this five-step approach:

1. **Declare your lens** — Acknowledge openly that you bring your own cultural perspective. "I want to name that I come from [background], and I may not fully understand your context. I will rely on you to guide me if something I say or ask does not land well."

2. **Ask, do not assume** — Instead of assuming you know how a client's culture shapes their perspective, ask open-ended questions: "How does your family's perspective influence this decision?" or "In your cultural context, what would be the expected approach here?"

3. **Explore the client's cultural narrative** — Invite the client to share how their cultural identity shapes the issue they are bringing. This is not about stereotyping but about understanding the client's lived experience.

4. **Adapt frameworks collaboratively** — Many coaching tools (SMART goals, wheel of life, values exercises) carry cultural assumptions. Ask the client: "Does this framework work for you, or would you like to modify it to fit your context better?"

5. **Hold cultural paradoxes** — A client may simultaneously hold values from their heritage culture and their adopted culture. Do not force them to choose. Hold space for the complexity.

## Religion and Spirituality in Coaching

Religion is a sensitive dimension of cross-cultural coaching. Some coaches avoid it entirely, worried about boundary violations. However, for many clients, spirituality is central to their identity and decision-making. The key is to follow the client's lead:

- Ask permission before exploring spiritual dimensions: "Is faith or spirituality something that plays into this area of your life?"
- Do not offer spiritual advice or interpret religious texts. Stay within your coaching scope.
- Be curious about how the client's religious framework informs their values, but do not impose your own beliefs.

If a client's religious context involves deference to religious authorities, respect that dynamic. You may need to include the client's spiritual advisor or adjust the coaching timeline to account for consultative decision-making processes.

---

## When You Get It Wrong

You will make mistakes. A culturally competent coach is not one who gets it right every time but one who repairs ruptures gracefully. If a client signals that you have made an assumption or caused offence:

1. Apologise simply and directly — no long explanations.
2. Thank the client for their trust in naming it.
3. Ask what they need from you going forward.
4. Adjust and move on.

Defensiveness is the enemy of cultural growth. If you find yourself explaining your intent, stop and listen instead.

## Assignment

1. Identify a cultural dimension or norm that differs from your own (e.g., attitudes toward time, family roles, directness of communication). Research it using at least two credible sources and write a 300-word reflection on how it might affect your coaching approach with a client from that background.

2. Write five open-ended questions you could use in a first coaching session with a client from a cultural background different from your own. These should be genuine curiosity questions, not leading questions.

3. Practise with a peer: roleplay a cross-cultural coaching scenario where you must declare your cultural lens and negotiate the coaching relationship. Write a 200-word reflection on what you noticed.
