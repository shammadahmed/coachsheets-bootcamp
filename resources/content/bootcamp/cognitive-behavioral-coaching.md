## What Is Cognitive Behavioral Coaching?

Cognitive Behavioral Coaching (CBC) adapts the principles of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) — one of the most empirically validated therapeutic approaches — for a non-clinical coaching context. CBC is based on the same foundational insight: **our thoughts influence our emotions, and our emotions influence our behaviors.** By changing the thought, we can change the feeling and the action.

But CBC is not CBT. The distinction is critical.

- **CBT** treats mental health disorders: clinical depression, generalized anxiety, panic disorder, OCD, PTSD. It is delivered by licensed therapists and may be covered by health insurance.
- **CBC** addresses everyday performance blockers: imposter syndrome, procrastination, negative self-talk, limiting beliefs about career or relationships. It is delivered by coaches with clients who are functioning well but want to function better.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Critical Ethical Boundary:** CBC is for helping functional clients overcome professional and personal blockers. It is NOT for treating anxiety disorders, depression, eating disorders, or any clinical condition. If you suspect a client has a clinical condition, you must refer them to a licensed mental health professional. Coaching is not therapy.

## The Cognitive Model in Coaching

The cognitive model can be visualized as a simple chain:

**Situation** → **Thought** → **Emotion** → **Behavior** → **Result**

Most clients believe the chain starts with the situation: "I feel anxious because my boss criticised me." The CBC insight is that the situation does not cause the emotion — the *thought about the situation* causes the emotion.

| **Situation** | **Thought** | **Emotion** | **Behavior** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boss says: "This report needs revision." | "I am terrible at my job. I am going to get fired." | Anxiety, shame | Avoid boss, procrastinate on revisions, consider quitting |
| Boss says: "This report needs revision." | "My boss is thorough. She wants this to be great." | Calm, motivated | Ask clarifying questions, revise, learn |

The situation is identical. The outcome is completely different — because the thought is different.

The coach's role is to help the client notice the automatic thought and examine it with curiosity rather than belief.

## Common Cognitive Distortions (In Coaching Contexts)

Cognitive distortions are systematic patterns of unhelpful thinking. In CBC, you help clients identify and label their distortions. Labelling a distortion creates distance from it — the client shifts from "This is true" to "This is a distortion I am experiencing."

### 1. All-or-Nothing Thinking (Black-and-White Thinking)
Seeing things in extreme categories with no middle ground.
- **Coaching example:** "I totally failed that presentation." (Reality: it had some good moments and some weak spots.)
- **Reframe:** "What parts went well? What one thing would you improve next time?"

### 2. Catastrophizing
Assuming the worst possible outcome will happen.
- **Coaching example:** "If I ask for a promotion, they will think I am arrogant and I will never advance here."
- **Reframe:** "What is the most likely outcome? What is the best-case outcome?"

### 3. Mind Reading
Assuming you know what others are thinking without evidence.
- **Coaching example:** "My team thinks I am incompetent."
- **Reframe:** "What evidence do you have? Is there another possibility?"

### 4. Overgeneralization
Taking one negative event as a never-ending pattern.
- **Coaching example:** "I messed up the budget once, so I am bad with numbers."
- **Reframe:** "What is one specific thing you can do to improve your budgeting skills?"

### 5. Should Statements (Must-urbation)
Rigid rules about how you or others "should" be.
- **Coaching example:** "I should be further along in my career by now."
- **Reframe:** "According to whose timeline? What would you say to a friend who said this?"

### 6. Emotional Reasoning
Believing that because you feel something, it must be true.
- **Coaching example:** "I feel like a fraud, so I must be a fraud."
- **Reframe:** "Feelings are not facts. What evidence contradicts this feeling?"

### 7. Disqualifying the Positive
Discounting positive experiences as "flukes" or "not counting."
- **Coaching example:** "Anyone could have closed that deal. It was an easy client."
- **Reframe:** "What skills did you use to close that deal? What did you contribute?"

### 8. Labeling
Attaching a global negative label to yourself or others.
- **Coaching example:** "I am a procrastinator." (vs. "I procrastinated on that one task.")
- **Reframe:** "Label yourself by your behavior, not your identity. What is a more accurate description?"

### 9. Personalization
Taking responsibility for events outside your control.
- **Coaching example:** "My team missed the deadline because I did not motivate them enough."
- **Reframe:** "What factors were in your control? What factors were not?"

### 10. Magnification/Minimization
Blowing things out of proportion or shrinking their importance.
- **Coaching example:** "This minor mistake is a huge disaster." (Magnification)
- **Reframe:** "How important will this be in one year? In five years?"

> [!WARNING]
> When a client presents with multiple persistent cognitive distortions that cause significant distress (difficulty getting out of bed, loss of appetite, social withdrawal, thoughts of self-harm), you are outside the scope of CBC. **Refer to a therapist immediately.**

## Using a Thought Record with Clients

The thought record is the most practical CBC tool. It helps clients slow down the automatic thought process and examine it objectively.

### The Five-Column Thought Record

| **Situation** | **Automatic Thought** | **Distortion** | **Reframed Thought** | **New Emotion** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What triggered it? | What went through your mind? | Which distortion(s)? | What is a more balanced thought? | How do you feel now? |

### Coaching Protocol for Thought Records

**Step 1:** Introduce the tool gently. "I would like to share a tool that helps us look at the thoughts that might be getting in the way. Would you be open to trying it?"

**Step 2:** Walk through the cognitive model using a real example from the client's week. Do not correct their thought — explore it.

**Step 3:** Help them identify the distortion. Use the list above as a reference. Clients often feel relieved when they can name the distortion — it externalizes the problem.

**Step 4:** Work together on a reframed thought. The reframe must feel believable to the client, not just "positive thinking." "I am the best employee ever" is not a good reframe. "I made one mistake on a report, and my overall performance has been strong" is a good reframe.

**Step 5:** Check the emotion shift. "How do you feel now, compared to before?" If the emotion has not shifted, the reframe may not be authentic enough. Try again.

**Step 6 (Homework):** Ask the client to complete one thought record per day between sessions for two weeks. Review together in the next session.

## Research Supporting CBC

CBC is a relatively young field, but the evidence base is growing. A 2015 meta-analysis published in the *Journal of Cognitive Behavioral Coaching* found that CBC interventions produced significant improvements in goal attainment, resilience, and self-efficacy compared to wait-list controls. Research by Anthony Grant at the University of Sydney — a leading figure in evidence-based coaching — has shown that cognitive-behavioral coaching techniques are effective for reducing procrastination, improving leadership performance, and enhancing psychological flexibility.

For a comprehensive overview, see: [CBC research at the Institute of Coaching](https://instituteofcoaching.org/resources/cognitive-behavioral-coaching).
