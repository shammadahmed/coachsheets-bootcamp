## What Is Motivational Interviewing?

Motivational Interviewing (MI) was developed by psychologists William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick in the 1980s as a method for addressing substance use disorders. It has since been validated across hundreds of clinical trials and adapted into coaching, healthcare, education, and management.

MI is defined as **a collaborative, goal-oriented style of communication with particular attention to the language of change.** It is designed to strengthen a person's own motivation and commitment to a specific goal by eliciting and exploring their own reasons for change.

The key insight: **people are more convinced by what they hear themselves say than by what others tell them.** A coach who tells a client "you should exercise more" creates resistance. A coach who asks "what would be different in your life if you exercised regularly?" helps the client articulate their own motivation.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> MI is **not a manipulation technique.** The goal is not to "make" the client want what the coach wants. The goal is to help the client discover and strengthen their *own* reasons for change, even if the coach would choose differently.

---

## The Spirit of MI: Four Key Elements

MI is not just a set of techniques — it is a way of being with clients. Miller and Rollnick identified four core elements of the MI spirit:

| **Element** | **Meaning** | **In Coaching Practice** |
|---|---|---|
| **Partnership** | Coach and client are collaborators, not expert and passive recipient | The coach does not prescribe; they explore together. "Let's look at this together." |
| **Acceptance** | Absolute worth, autonomy, accurate empathy, affirmation | The client has the right to not change. The coach respects that without judgment. |
| **Compassion** | The coach actively promotes the client's welfare | Decisions about what to pursue are made in the client's best interest, not the coach's. |
| **Evocation** | The client already has the resources and motivation inside them | The coach's job is to draw out, not pour in. "What do you already know about this?" |

## OARS: The Core Skills (Deepened)

You were introduced to OARS in Module 1. Here we deepen the practice with specific attention to MI applications.

### Open Questions
Open questions invite exploration rather than yes/no answers. In MI, open questions are designed to elicit "change talk" — the client's own statements about their desire, ability, reasons, and need for change.

- Closed: "Do you want to change careers?" (Answer: yes/no — conversation ends)
- Open: "What makes you consider a career change at this point in your life?" (Answer: narrative — conversation opens)

### Affirmations
Affirmations are genuine statements of recognition and appreciation. They are not flattery. An affirmation notices and names a client's strengths.

- "That took courage to admit."
- "I can see how much thought you have already given to this."
- "It is not easy to sit with uncertainty, and you are doing it."

### Reflections
Reflections are the most powerful MI skill and the hardest to master. A reflection is a statement (not a question) that captures something the client said or implied. Reflections can be:

- **Simple:** Restating what the client said. "You feel stuck in your current role."
- **Complex:** Adding meaning or inference. "You feel stuck, and it seems like part of you is ready to leave and part of you is scared of what comes next."

**The reflection rule:** Use at least two reflections for every question you ask. Most new coaches ask too many questions and reflect too little.

### Summaries
Summaries pull together what the client has said, demonstrating listening and helping the client see the big picture. A good summary ends with an invitation: "Did I get that right? What else?"

## The Stages of Change (Prochaska & DiClemente)

The Transtheoretical Model of Change, developed by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente in the 1970s, describes change as a process with six stages. Clients cycle through these stages — often non-linearly — as they work toward any meaningful change.

### Stage 1: Precontemplation
The client is not considering change. They may be unaware of the problem or in denial. In coaching, this looks like a client who was sent by their manager or partner.

- **Coach's job:** Build rapport, raise awareness without pressure.
- **Avoid:** Pushing for action. The client is not ready.
- **Question:** "What would need to happen for you to consider this might be worth exploring?"

### Stage 2: Contemplation
The client is ambivalent — aware of the need for change but not committed. "I should exercise more, but..."

- **Coach's job:** Explore ambivalence. Use decisional balance: "What are the pros and cons of changing? Of not changing?"
- **Avoid:** Leaping to action planning. The client is still weighing options.

### Stage 3: Preparation
The client has decided to change and is getting ready. "I am going to start exercising next week."

- **Coach's job:** Help them plan. Identify specific steps, resources, and potential obstacles.
- **Question:** "What would need to be in place for you to succeed?"

### Stage 4: Action
The client is actively making changes. This is visible, observable behavior change.

- **Coach's job:** Support, affirm, troubleshoot. "What is working? What is harder than expected?"
- **Avoid:** Assuming the change is permanent. Action is fragile.

### Stage 5: Maintenance
The client has sustained the change for more than 6 months. The new behavior is becoming habitual.

- **Coach's job:** Reinforce, prevent relapse, integrate the change into identity.
- **Question:** "How has this change changed how you see yourself?"

### Stage 6: Relapse
The client returns to old behavior. This is normal and expected — not failure.

- **Coach's job:** Normalize, learn, recommit. "What did you learn from this? What would you do differently?"
- **Crucial message:** Relapse is a stage, not a failure. Most people cycle through stages 3–6 multiple times before change sticks.

> [!WARNING]
> Do not push a client into Action before they are ready. Coaches often rush to action because it feels productive. But a client in Contemplation who is pushed into Action will likely relapse quickly and feel like a failure. Meet the client where they are.

## Applying MI in Coaching (Not Therapy)

MI was developed for clinical settings, but it transfers directly to coaching with **three key adaptations:**

### 1. Change is self-defined, not health-defined
In therapy, the change target is often clinical (reduce drinking, manage depression). In coaching, the client defines what "change" means — career shift, habit building, relationship dynamics. The coach follows the client's agenda.

### 2. Ambivalence is normal, not pathological
Therapy often treats ambivalence as a symptom to be resolved. Coaching treats ambivalence as a natural part of any meaningful decision. The coach holds space for "both/and" thinking: "Part of you wants to stay, and part of you wants to leave. Both are valid."

### 3. No diagnosis, no label
MI in therapy may involve diagnostic labels (alcohol use disorder, depression). In coaching, you never label. You describe behavior: "You mentioned you have been skipping workouts" — not "You have an exercise avoidance problem."

## Change Talk vs. Sustain Talk

In MI, the coach listens for two types of client language:

- **Change talk:** Statements that move toward change. "I need to..." "I wish I could..." "I am ready to..."
- **Sustain talk:** Statements that maintain the status quo. "It is not that bad." "I tried before and failed." "I am too busy."

The coach's job is to **elicit and reinforce change talk** while **not fighting sustain talk.** Arguing with sustain talk ("But you just said you wanted to change!") creates resistance. Instead, reflect the sustain talk empathetically and gently explore the other side.

"Sustain talk: 'I don't have time to exercise.'
Reflection: 'Time is really tight for you right now, and adding exercise feels like one more demand. And yet, you mentioned earlier that your energy is low and you think exercise would help. How do you hold both of those?'"
