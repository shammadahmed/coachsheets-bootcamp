# Coaching vs. Therapy: Ethical Boundaries

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- Distinguish coaching from therapy along legal, ethical, and practical lines
- Identify red-flag client statements that require referral to a licensed mental-health professional
- Conduct a respectful referral conversation without damaging the coaching relationship
- Navigate the common grey zone of clients who see both a therapist and a coach
- Respond appropriately when trauma surfaces during a coaching session
- Apply the ICF Code of Ethics standards related to scope of practice

---

## Why This Distinction Matters

The coaching profession operates in a unique space. Unlike therapy, which is a regulated healthcare field with licensure boards, liability standards, and diagnostic frameworks (DSM-5-TR, ICD-11), coaching is largely self-regulated. That freedom is also its greatest ethical vulnerability.

When coaches blur the line between coaching and therapy -- even unintentionally -- they expose themselves to legal liability (practicing without a license), ethical complaints to bodies like the International Coaching Federation (ICF), and, most importantly, harm to the client. A client who needs clinical treatment will not get better through coaching alone, and a coach who tries to "treat" a clinical condition is doing real damage.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Coaching is not a substitute for therapy. If you are not a licensed mental-health professional, you must never diagnose, treat, or counsel pathology. Your job is to work with *functional* clients who want *growth*.

---

## The Scope-of-Practice Boundary: Growth vs. Pathology

A useful shorthand comes from the ICF: coaching works with **healthy, functional individuals who want to move from good to great** (or who are stuck and want to reach a specific goal). Therapy treats **pathology, disorder, and dysfunction** -- conditions that impair daily functioning.

| Domain | Coaching | Therapy |
|--------|----------|---------|
| Focus | Present + future goals, performance, growth | Past + present healing, symptom reduction |
| Client state | Functioning well, wants more | Struggling to function, wants relief |
| What is "diagnosed"? | Clarified goals, values, strengths | Mental-health disorders (DSM-5-TR) |
| Relationship | Collaborative peer (coach does not need more expertise in the client's life than the client) | Expert-to-client (clinician diagnoses and treats) |
| Duration | Short-to-medium term (often 3-12 sessions) | Variable (can be months to years) |
| Outcome | Measurable goal achievement | Symptom remission, improved functioning |

The ICF makes this explicit in its Code of Ethics: _"I will recognize that my work as a coach is distinct from therapy, and that I will refer clients to other professionals as appropriate."_

## Red Flags That Require Referral

Certain statements or behaviours are clear signals that a client needs a referral to a licensed therapist. If you hear anything resembling the list below, **do not continue coaching on that topic**:

- **Suicidal ideation**: "I've been thinking about ending things." / "I don't see the point of going on."
- **Clinical depression**: "I haven't been able to get out of bed for two weeks." / "Nothing brings me joy anymore."
- **Anxiety disorders**: "I can't leave the house without checking the lock 20 times." / "I have panic attacks daily."
- **Trauma / PTSD**: Flashbacks, intrusive memories, hypervigilance linked to a past event.
- **Addiction**: "I can't stop drinking / using / gambling even though it's ruining my life."
- **Eating disorders**: Restrictive eating, bingeing, purging, or body dysmorphia.
- **Self-harm**: Cutting, burning, or other deliberate physical harm.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> If a client discloses suicidal ideation, you must take it seriously. Ask directly: "Are you thinking about harming yourself?" Do not leave them alone. Provide crisis resources immediately: **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** (in the US) or your local crisis number. Document everything.

### The Threshold Test

When in doubt, ask yourself: **"Is this person struggling to function in their daily life?"** If the answer is yes -- they can't hold a job, maintain relationships, or manage basic self-care -- they need a therapist, not a coach.

## The Grey Zone: Clients With Both a Coach and a Therapist

Many clients **already see a therapist** and want a coach to work on a specific life area (career, fitness, organisation). This is common and can be productive -- as long as boundaries are clear.

**When it works well:**

- The therapist handles clinical issues (anxiety, depression, trauma).
- The coach works on behavioural goals (build a morning routine, start a business, improve public speaking).
- The client understands the distinction and keeps both roles informed (with appropriate release forms).

**When it becomes problematic:**

- The client tries to use coaching hours to process therapy material.
- The coach starts offering opinions on the client's diagnosis or medication.
- The two roles blur: the coach begins acting as an "unofficial therapist."

**Best practice:** Ask every new client during the intake call, "Are you currently seeing a therapist or other mental-health professional?" If yes, clarify: "My role as your coach is to help you achieve [specific goal]. If a conversation starts to drift into clinical territory, I may pause and suggest we discuss it with your therapist instead."

## What To Do When Trauma Surfaces Mid-Session

Despite your best screening, trauma can surface unexpectedly. A coaching question like "What stops you from pursuing that promotion?" can trigger a memory of workplace abuse. When this happens:

1. **Pause coaching immediately.** Stop asking forward-moving questions.
2. **Express care.** "I can see this is bringing up something significant. Thank you for trusting me with it."
3. **Name the boundary.** "What you are describing sounds like it could benefit from professional support. As your coach, I am not trained to work with trauma -- but I want to make sure you get the right help."
4. **Refer.** Have a short list of licensed therapists or low-cost counselling resources ready.
5. **Check in.** "Would you like me to follow up with you in a few days to see how you are doing?"

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Never try to "coach through" trauma. It is outside your scope unless you are a licensed trauma therapist wearing your coaching hat for a separate purpose. The ethical duty is to refer.

## ICF Code of Ethics References

The ICF Code of Ethics (2024 revision) contains several standards directly relevant to scope of practice:

- **Standard 1.5**: "I will recognize that my work as a coach is distinct from therapy, and that I will refer clients to other professionals as appropriate."
- **Standard 1.6**: "I will accurately identify my level of competence and will not misrepresent my qualifications or expertise."
- **Standard 1.7**: "I will avoid offering professional information or advice that I know is inaccurate, outside my scope of practice, or beyond my level of competence."
- **Standard 2.4**: "I will maintain the strictest levels of confidentiality with all client and sponsor information."

Read the full code at: [https://coachingfederation.org/ethics](https://coachingfederation.org/ethics)
