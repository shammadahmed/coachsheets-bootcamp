# Module 4, Lesson 3: Post-Session Notes and Between-Session Work

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Write effective session notes that capture themes, shifts, and action items without excessive detail
- Use CoachSheets' session notes feature to organize and reference past sessions
- Design between-session assignments that are specific, meaningful, and appropriate in volume
- Distinguish between useful documentation and subjective over-documentation

---

## Why Post-Session Notes Matter

The session is over. The client hangs up, walks out, or closes the video call. In the 10 minutes that follow, you have a window of opportunity to capture what matters before it fades. Post-session notes serve three critical functions:

1. **Continuity.** Your notes are the bridge between one session and the next. Without them, each session starts from zero rather than building on accumulated work.
2. **Accountability.** Written commitments are more likely to be remembered—by both you and the client.
3. **Professional development.** Reviewing your session notes over time reveals your own patterns as a coach: where you tend to focus, what you miss, how your clients progress.

A 2022 survey by the European Mentoring and Coaching Council found that coaches who maintained structured session notes reported significantly higher client retention and perceived effectiveness. Notes are not paperwork—they are a coaching tool.

---

## What to Record

Effective session notes are structured and concise. Here is what to capture:

### Themes

What was the central thread of the session? Write one or two sentences that capture the core theme. Examples:

- "Client explored pattern of taking on others' emotions in team settings."
- "Breakthrough: client connected fear of visibility to childhood experience of being told to 'stay small.'"

Themes are useful because they make it easy to see continuity (or discontinuity) across sessions. If you look at three consecutive notes and see completely unrelated themes, that is data worth examining.

### Shifts

Did the client say something that represented a change in perspective, energy, or self-understanding? Capture it in the client's own words if possible:

- "Client said: 'For the first time, I realize I'm not actually afraid of the promotion. I'm afraid of being happy about wanting it.'"
- "Shift in energy mid-session — client moved from defensive posture to openness when we connected leadership anxiety to values."

Shifts are precious in coaching. They are the moments when learning crystallizes. Capturing them in notes lets you reference them in future sessions: "Last time you had a real shift around your relationship with ambition. How has that landed since we talked?"

### Action Items

What did the client commit to? Be specific:

- "Client will sit down with partner this week and discuss dividing household responsibilities using the conversation template from session 3."
- "Client will complete the Values Compass worksheet before next session."

Include deadlines or timeframes. A commitment without a timeframe is a wish, not an action.

### Observations

What did you notice as the coach that may or may not have been surfaced in the session? These are for your eyes only:

- "Client seemed more tired than usual — worth checking in on sleep/burnout next session."
- "Noticed client used 'I should' language more than 10 times in 45 minutes."

Observations are hypotheses, not facts. Hold them lightly.

## What NOT to Record

Just as important as what to include is what to leave out. Poor session notes are a liability.

### Excessive Detail

You do not need to transcribe the session. A 45-minute session should produce notes of 150-300 words, not 1,500. Detailed notes eat up your time between clients and make it harder to find what matters when you review them later. If you find yourself writing long paragraphs, you are documenting instead of thinking.

### Subjective Judgments Stated as Fact

Avoid labeling clients in your notes. "Client was resistant" is a judgment, not an observation. Instead, describe what happened: "Client dismissed three suggestions for next steps without offering alternatives." The first version closes down your curiosity; the second opens it.

Similarly, avoid pathologizing language: "Client seems depressed," "Client is avoidant." Unless you are a licensed therapist making a clinical assessment, stick to behavioral observations. This protects both you and the client.

### Personal Opinions

Your notes are not a diary. "I felt frustrated with the client today" is better explored in supervision than stored in session notes. If your emotional reaction to a client is relevant, bring it to your supervisor or coach, not your note-taking system.

## Using CoachSheets Session Notes Feature

CoachSheets includes a dedicated session notes interface. Here is how to use it effectively:

1. **Create the session record during or immediately after the call.** Navigate to the client's profile and add a new session entry.
2. **Use the structured fields.** CoachSheets provides separate fields for themes, observations, action items, and next session focus. Use them as intended — they are designed to help you write better notes, not just store them.
3. **Tag relevant worksheets or submissions.** If a particular worksheet was central to the session, link it. This creates a searchable history you can reference months later.
4. **Review before the next session.** Before your next session with this client, review your previous notes. This takes 2-3 minutes and dramatically improves session continuity.

The CoachSheets session notes feature also feeds into the client timeline, giving you a chronological view of your work together. This is invaluable for long-term coaching relationships.

## Assigning Between-Session Work

Between-session assignments are one of the most powerful tools in coaching. They extend the impact of the session into the client's daily life and create material for the next conversation.

### What to Assign

Good between-session work falls into one of three categories:

- **Reflection:** Journaling prompts, worksheets, values clarification exercises. These deepen self-awareness. Example: "This week, notice how many times you say yes to something you don't want to do. Just notice — don't change anything yet."
- **Action:** Behavioral experiments, conversations, practice of new skills. These translate insight into behavior change. Example: "Before your team meeting on Wednesday, prepare one question that invites input from the quietest person in the room. Ask it."
- **Observation:** Data gathering without intervention. Example: "Track your energy levels every two hours for three days. Rate 1-10. Bring the data to our next session."

### How to Frame It

The framing of between-session work matters as much as the content:

- **CO-create it.** Do not assign. Ask: "What would be a useful experiment to run between now and our next session?"
- **Make it meaningful.** The client should understand why this particular assignment will serve their goals.
- **Keep it small.** One assignment is usually enough. Two is the maximum. More than that and the client will feel overwhelmed and likely do none of them.
- **Connect it to the session.** "Based on what we discovered today about how you avoid conflict, what feels like the smallest next step?"

### How Much Is Too Much?

A good rule of thumb: the between-session work should take no more than 20-30 minutes total. If a worksheet would take an hour, break it into parts or reconsider whether it is appropriate.

Signs you are assigning too much:
- The client repeatedly arrives with incomplete assignments
- The client apologizes for not doing the work (this is your cue to adjust, not to reassure)
- The client seems overwhelmed or anxious when discussing between-session work
- You are spending more than 5 minutes in each session reviewing assignments

When a client consistently does not complete assignments, it is rarely laziness. Consider: is the assignment meaningful? Is it the right size? Is the timing right? Does the client fully understand the purpose? Non-completion is data, not failure.

## Summary

Post-session notes are a professional discipline that supports continuity, accountability, and your own growth as a coach. Record themes, shifts, action items, and observations. Skip excessive detail, subjective judgments, and personal opinions. Use CoachSheets' structured fields to keep notes organized and searchable. For between-session work, co-create meaningful assignments that are small enough to actually get done. When a client does not complete assignments, treat it as data about the assignment, not the client.
