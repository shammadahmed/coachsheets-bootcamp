# Lesson 6.5: Group Dynamics and Team Coaching

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- Distinguish between team coaching, group coaching, and facilitation
- Apply Tuckman's stages of group development to coaching interventions
- Facilitate group coaching sessions that balance participation and psychological safety
- Manage challenging group dynamics including dominant and quiet participants
- Design a group coaching programme with appropriate norms, structure, and boundaries

---

## Team Coaching vs. Group Coaching vs. Facilitation

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they describe distinct practices with different goals, structures, and competencies.

**Group coaching** involves multiple participants who share a common context or goal (e.g., a group of new managers, a group of entrepreneurs) but who do not necessarily work together. The coach works with individuals within the group context, and participants learn from each other's experiences. The group is a container for individual growth.

**Team coaching** involves a real team — a group of people who are interdependent and share accountability for collective outcomes (e.g., a leadership team, a project team). The coach's client is the team itself, not the individuals within it. The goal is to improve the team's collective performance, relationships, and processes.

**Facilitation** guides a group through a process (a meeting, a strategic planning session, a retrospective) toward a specific output. Unlike coaching, facilitation does not aim to build the group's long-term capacity or insight — it aims to produce a result.

| Practice | Primary Client | Goal | Coach Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group coaching | Individuals in the group | Individual growth within group context | Coach + process guide |
| Team coaching | The team as a system | Collective performance | Observer + intervener |
| Facilitation | The task/outcome | Specific output | Process guide |

## Tuckman's Stages of Group Development

Bruce Tuckman's model (1965, extended 1977) describes how groups evolve. Each stage requires different coaching interventions.

### 1. Forming
The group comes together. Members are polite, uncertain, and looking for direction. They want to know: Do I belong here? Can I trust these people? What are the rules?

**Coaching interventions:**
- Establish clear group norms and agreements
- Provide structure and clarity about purpose, logistics, and expectations
- Facilitation of low-risk introductions and icebreakers
- Explicit discussion of confidentiality, participation expectations, and group values

### 2. Storming
Conflict emerges. Members push against boundaries, challenge each other and the coach, and compete for role and influence. This is the stage where most groups fail or become stuck.

**Coaching interventions:**
- Normalise conflict as healthy and expected. "This is a sign the group is developing."
- Stay neutral. Do not take sides in group conflicts.
- Teach the group conflict resolution skills: "I" statements, paraphrasing, checking assumptions.
- Hold the group's capacity to stay in conversation without rescuing them from discomfort.
- Set the boundary that the coach's role is not to solve conflicts but to support the group in solving them.

### 3. Norming
The group establishes norms, roles, and ways of working together. Trust begins to build. Members start to identify with the group.

**Coaching interventions:**
- Reinforce emerging positive norms
- Help the group articulate its shared values and agreements
- Encourage deeper sharing and risk-taking
- Begin shifting more ownership to the group

### 4. Performing
The group functions effectively. Members trust each other, communicate openly, and achieve results. The coach's presence is less central.

**Coaching interventions:**
- Challenge the group to stretch further
- Introduce more complex coaching models
- Encourage peer coaching within the group
- Step back significantly; the group co-creates its own process

### 5. Adjourning (or Mourning)
The group disbands or transitions. Members may experience loss, celebrate accomplishments, or feel uncertain about what comes next.

**Coaching interventions:**
- Facilitate closing rituals and celebration
- Support reflection on individual and collective learning
- Help members articulate how they will carry forward their growth

## Psychological Safety in Groups

Amy Edmondson's research at Harvard identified psychological safety as the single most important factor in effective team learning and performance. Psychological safety is the belief that one can speak up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes without being punished or humiliated.

### Building Psychological Safety in Coaching Groups

- **Model vulnerability.** The coach admitting their own uncertainty or mistakes signals that it is safe to be imperfect.
- **Establish confidentiality explicitly.** "What is said in this room stays in this room. What is learned leaves."
- **Interrupt power dynamics.** Be aware of differences in seniority, status, experience, and identity within the group. Create structures that give everyone voice.
- **Use rounds.** Go around the circle so everyone speaks, not just those who volunteer. This prevents dominant voices from filling the space.
- **Respond well to difficult disclosures.** When a member shares something vulnerable, acknowledge it with warmth and move on without dwelling. Over-attention can discourage future sharing as much as under-attention.

## Managing Dominant and Quiet Participants

### The Dominant Participant
- Use time-bound rounds that give everyone equal airtime.
- Name the pattern in a neutral way: "I am noticing you have shared three times already. Let me hear from someone who has not spoken yet."
- If the pattern persists, have a private conversation: "I value your contribution, and I need to make sure everyone's voice is heard. Could we agree that you will count to three before speaking next time?"
- Design activities that require written reflection before verbal sharing (write first, then share).

### The Quiet Participant
- Do not pressure or put on the spot. Create low-stakes ways to participate.
- Use pair-share before large group sharing (let them test their voice with one person first).
- Provide opt-out options: "If you want to pass, that is always okay."
- Use written contributions (sticky notes, shared documents, chat) alongside verbal ones.
- Check in privately to understand the cause: is it introversion, cultural norms, lack of safety, or something else?

## Designing a Group Coaching Programme

**Group size:** 4-8 participants is optimal for coaching groups. Larger groups limit individual airtime and depth.

**Session structure:**
1. Check-in (5-10 min) — How is everyone arriving?
2. Topic input (10-15 min) — Brief teaching or framing
3. Individual reflection (5-10 min) — Journaling or worksheet
4. Paired or small group work (15-20 min) — Deeper exploration
5. Full group coaching (20-30 min) — Coach works with individuals or the group
6. Action and accountability (5-10 min) — Commitments for the week
7. Check-out (5 min) — One word or one insight

**Duration:** Most group coaching programmes run 6-12 sessions over 8-16 weeks.

**Boundaries:**
- Clear attendance policy
- Confidentiality agreement
- Opt-out from specific exercises (especially high-vulnerability ones)
- The coach's availability between sessions (defined, not open-ended)
- Referral policy if a participant needs individual support

## Ethical Considerations in Group Coaching

- **Confidentiality:** You can promise it from your side but cannot guarantee other participants will maintain it. Be transparent about this.
- **Dual relationships:** Be careful about coaching individuals within a team you also coach as a team. The coaching relationship with the individual may conflict with what the team needs.
- **Emotional safety:** Group coaching can trigger strong emotions. Have a protocol for checking in with participants who show distress and know your referral resources.
- **Boundary between coaching and therapy:** In a group setting, this boundary can blur. Screen participants before the group starts and be clear about what the group is and is not.

## Assignment

1. Identify a group you are currently part of or have been part of (work team, sports team, volunteer group). Map its development against Tuckman's model. Write 300 words describing which stages you observed and what behaviours marked each stage.

2. Design a 6-session group coaching programme outline. For each session, specify: (a) the topic, (b) one activity, (c) one coaching question you would use with the group, and (d) how you would address the relevant Tuckman stage.

3. Write a group coaching agreement document (200-300 words) covering: confidentiality, participation expectations, attendance, communication outside sessions, and the coach's role and limits.

4. Practise facilitating a group coaching round on a low-stakes topic with 3-5 peers. Write a 200-word reflection: How did you manage airtime? Did anyone dominate or stay quiet? What would you do differently?
