## Why Goal-Setting Matters in Coaching

Goal-setting is one of the most robustly studied topics in behavioural psychology. Over 50 years of research shows that specific, challenging goals lead to higher performance than vague intentions like "do your best."

For coaching specifically, goals serve three critical functions:

1. **Direction.** A clear goal answers "Where are we going?" Without one, sessions drift.
2. **Accountability.** The coach holds space between sessions. A specific commitment increases follow-through.
3. **Progress measurement.** Goals let both coach and client see whether coaching is working. If the client is moving toward the goal, the relationship is productive. If not, it is time to re-evaluate.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Goals are not the same as tasks. A goal is a desired outcome. A task is a step toward it. Coaches help clients set goals *and* break them into tasks. Both are necessary.

## SMART Goals: The Classic Framework

SMART is the most widely known goal-setting framework. The most common version is:

| Letter | Meaning | Coaching Question |
|--------|---------|-------------------|
| **S** | Specific | What exactly do you want to achieve? |
| **M** | Measurable | How will you know you have achieved it? |
| **A** | Achievable | Is this realistic for you right now? |
| **R** | Relevant | Does this align with your values and priorities? |
| **T** | Time-bound | By when will you achieve it? |

### Concrete Coaching Example

A client says: "I want to be healthier." That is a wish, not a goal. Here is the SMART version after coaching:

- **Specific:** "I will strength-train three times per week."
- **Measurable:** "I will track each session in a log and measure my consistency rate."
- **Achievable:** "I currently exercise zero times per week. Three times is a stretch but doable with gym access."
- **Relevant:** "I value vitality. This goal supports my identity as someone who takes care of their body."
- **Time-bound:** "I will maintain this habit for 12 consecutive weeks, by November 1st."

**Final SMART goal:** "I will strength-train three times per week for 12 weeks, tracking each session, to establish a consistent fitness habit."

## Limitations of SMART Goals

Despite its popularity, SMART has blind spots every coach should know:

1. **Too rigid.** A SMART goal set in January may be irrelevant by March. The framework does not accommodate iteration.
2. **Ignores motivation.** SMART assumes a well-defined goal will be pursued. It does not address what happens when motivation drops or obstacles arise.
3. **Focuses on outcome over process.** Many SMART goals are outcome-oriented (lose 10 kg, earn $50k). But outcomes are partially outside the client's control.
4. **Does not surface obstacles.** SMART asks if a goal is "achievable" but does not systematically explore the barriers in the way.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> SMART is a good starting point, but it is rarely sufficient on its own. The best coaches use SMART as one tool in a larger kit, combined with frameworks that address motivation, obstacles, and the psychology of follow-through.

## WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan

Developed by psychologist Gabriele Oettingen at New York University, WOOP is based on decades of research on **mental contrasting** -- juxtaposing a desired future with the current reality that blocks it.

The research is striking: positive thinking alone (just visualising success) actually *decreases* effort and follow-through. But mental contrasting -- visualising the desired outcome *and* the obstacle -- increases action. WOOP operationalises this finding.

### The Four Steps

**W -- Wish.** What is a meaningful, challenging wish you have? (E.g., "I want to start my own coaching practice.")

**O -- Outcome.** What is the best outcome? Close your eyes and imagine it vividly. (E.g., "I feel fulfilled helping others. My income grows.")

**O -- Obstacle.** What is the main internal obstacle inside *you* that holds you back? (E.g., "I procrastinate on marketing because I am afraid of rejection.")

**P -- Plan.** Make an if-then plan to overcome the obstacle. (E.g., "If it is Tuesday at 9 AM, then I will send one networking email, even if I feel nervous.")

> [!IMPORTANT]
> WOOP is most powerful for clients who *know what they want* but *struggle to act*. If a client says "I know what to do, I just do not do it," WOOP is often more useful than SMART.

### When to Use WOOP vs. SMART

- **SMART** is best when the client needs clarity: they do not know exactly what they want or how to define it.
- **WOOP** is best when the client needs activation: they know the goal but are stuck in avoidance, procrastination, or fear.

The two frameworks complement each other: SMART to clarify the goal, then WOOP to address the obstacle.

## Implementation Intentions: "If-Then" Plans

Implementation intentions were developed by psychologist Peter Gollwitzer. They follow a simple formula:

> **If [situation], then I will [behaviour].**

The research shows that implementation intentions dramatically increase follow-through. A meta-analysis of 94 studies found a medium-to-large effect size (d = 0.65) on goal achievement.

### Why They Work

The "if" part creates a mental trigger. When the situation arises, the behaviour fires automatically -- bypassing deliberation and hesitation. The plan shifts from conscious effort to habit-like automaticity.

### Coaching Example

A client who wants to exercise more but struggles with procrastination might create: "If it is Monday, Wednesday, or Friday at 6:30 AM, then I will put on my running shoes and go for a 30-minute jog."

When coaching a client, help them identify their obstacle (the "if") and pair it with a concrete action (the "then").

## Outcome Goals vs. Process Goals

| Type | Definition | Example | Controllable? |
|------|-----------|---------|---------------|
| **Outcome goal** | A desired end result | "Lose 10 kg in 3 months" | Partially |
| **Process goal** | A behaviour or habit you commit to | "Exercise 4 times per week" | Fully |

**Why this matters:** Outcome goals are motivating but can discourage if the result does not come. Process goals are fully within the client's control, building confidence and momentum.

**Best practice:** Set both. Outcome goals for direction, process goals for daily action.

## Locke and Latham's Goal-Setting Theory

Locke and Latham published their landmark goal-setting theory in 1990, synthesising decades of research. The core findings:

1. **Specific goals produce higher performance than vague goals.** "Increase sales by 15%" beats "do your best to increase sales."
2. **Challenging goals produce higher performance than easy goals.** But the goal must still be achievable (the "stretch zone").
3. **Feedback is essential.** Goals without feedback are like driving with your eyes closed.
4. **Goal commitment matters.** The client must believe the goal is important and achievable.
5. **Task complexity modifies the effect.** For complex tasks, shorter-term sub-goals are more effective than a single long-term goal.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Locke and Latham's most practical finding: "Do your best" is the worst possible goal instruction. Always prefer specific, measurable targets.
