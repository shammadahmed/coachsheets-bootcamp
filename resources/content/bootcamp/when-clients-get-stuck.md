# Module 4, Lesson 6: When Clients Get Stuck

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Recognize the difference between a coaching plateau, resistance, and a clinical issue requiring referral
- Apply the Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change) to understand where a client is in their readiness
- Decide when to change your coaching approach and when to sit with the stuckness
- Interpret non-completion of worksheets as data about the worksheet, the timing, or the client's readiness

---

## The Reality of Stuckness

Every coach encounters stuck clients. It is not a sign of failure—it is a sign that coaching is touching something real. If clients never got stuck, they would not need coaches. The question is not *whether* clients will get stuck, but *how* you will respond when they do.

Stuckness takes many forms:

- **Plateaus:** Progress has stalled. The client is showing up, doing the work, but nothing seems to change.
- **Resistance:** The client pushes back against worksheets, suggestions, or the coaching process itself.
- **Non-completion:** Worksheets go undone. Commitments are not kept. Sessions are rescheduled.
- **Surface compliance:** The client says all the right things but nothing shifts beneath the surface.
- **Cycling:** The same themes appear session after session with no observable evolution.

Each of these requires a different response. The skill is in accurate assessment and pattern recognition.

> "The only way out is through." — Robert Frost

## The Stages of Change Model

The Transtheoretical Model of Behavior Change (Prochaska and DiClemente, 1983) is one of the most useful frameworks for understanding client stuckness. It describes change as a process with distinct stages:

### 1. Precontemplation
The client is not considering change. They may be unaware there is a problem, or they may have tried and failed before and given up. In coaching, this is rare (clients typically seek coaching because they want change), but it can appear when a client's partner, employer, or family pushed them into coaching.

**Coaching response:** Build awareness. Do not push for action. Ask questions that gently surface the gap between current state and desired future.

### 2. Contemplation
The client is aware of the problem and thinking about change, but has not committed to action. "I know I need to delegate more, but I'm not sure I'm ready."

**Coaching response:** Explore ambivalence. Use decisional balance exercises. Do not rush to action planning—the client is not ready.

### 3. Preparation
The client intends to take action soon and may have begun small steps. "I'm going to talk to my manager about flexible hours next week."

**Coaching response:** Support planning. Help the client identify specific, achievable first steps. This is the stage where worksheets and action plans are most effective.

### 4. Action
The client is actively implementing changes. "I had the conversation. It went better than I expected."

**Coaching response:** Reinforce, celebrate, and troubleshoot. The client needs encouragement and help navigating obstacles.

### 5. Maintenance
The client has sustained the change for some time and is working to prevent relapse. "It's been three months since I started delegating consistently. I still slip sometimes when I'm stressed."

**Coaching response:** Support sustainability. Identify triggers for relapse. Help the client integrate the change into their identity.

### 6. Relapse (or Recycling)
The client has returned to an earlier stage. This is normal, not failure. Most clients cycle through stages multiple times before sustained change.

**Coaching response:** Normalize. Learn from the relapse. Adjust the approach and re-enter the cycle at the appropriate stage.

### Applying the Model

When a client is stuck, ask: *What stage of change are they actually in?* Many coaches make the mistake of treating contemplators as if they are in preparation—pushing for action plans when the client is still ambivalent. This creates resistance. Match your coaching approach to the client's actual stage, not where you wish they were.

## When to Change Approach vs. When to Sit with Stuckness

This is one of the hardest judgments in coaching. Here is a framework for deciding.

### Change Your Approach When:

- **The current approach has had 3-4 sessions with no observable shift.** Try a different framework, a different worksheet type, or a different conversation structure.
- **The client has explicitly said the current approach is not working.** Listen to them. They know themselves.
- **The client's engagement is dropping.** Declining completion rates, rescheduled sessions, or shorter worksheet answers suggest the current approach is not landing.
- **You have a hypothesis that a different framework would serve better.** If you are holding back a tool because you want to finish what you started, let it go. Try the new thing.

### Sit with Stuckness When:

- **The client is in a natural plateau.** Change is not linear. Periods of consolidation are normal and necessary.
- **The stuckness itself is the material.** Sometimes the most productive coaching conversation is about why the client feels stuck. "What is it like for you to feel stuck right now?" can be more powerful than any worksheet.
- **The client has recently had a breakthrough.** After a significant shift, a period of integration is appropriate. Do not pathologize a pause.
- **The client needs to feel held in their stuckness.** For some clients, the experience of being accepted exactly where they are—without a coach trying to fix them—is itself transformative.

## When Non-Completion Is Data

When a client does not complete a worksheet, it is easy to label them as "unmotivated" or "not ready for coaching." Resist this impulse. Non-completion is data. The question is: data about *what*?

### The Worksheet Itself Is Wrong

- The worksheet is too long or too time-consuming
- The worksheet asks questions the client does not find relevant
- The worksheet format does not suit the client's learning style (e.g., a visual client given a text-heavy worksheet)
- The worksheet touches on something the client is not ready to explore

**What to do:** Review the worksheet. Ask the client for feedback. Offer an alternative.

### The Timing Is Wrong

- The client is in a particularly busy or stressful period
- The worksheet was assigned too soon after a heavy session
- The client had a significant life event (illness, family crisis, work deadline)

**What to do:** Adjust the schedule. Reduce assignment volume temporarily. Offer extensions without shame.

### The Client Isn't Ready

- The client is in precontemplation or contemplation and is being pushed into action
- The coaching focus does not align with the client's actual priorities
- The client's goals are not their own—they were set by a boss, partner, or social expectation

**What to do:** Go back to goal setting. Have an honest conversation about what the client actually wants. Consider whether the coaching relationship is properly aligned.

## When to Refer to Therapy

This is one of the most important boundaries in coaching. Coaching is not therapy. Coaches work with well-functioning individuals who want to move from good to great, or who are facing normal life challenges. When a client's stuckness reflects a clinical issue, the ethical response is to refer.

### Signs That Referral May Be Appropriate

- **Persistent low mood or anhedonia** that lasts weeks, not days
- **Suicidal ideation or self-harm** (refer immediately; follow your safeguarding protocol)
- **Substance use that impairs daily functioning**
- **Trauma symptoms** that are being triggered by coaching work
- **Eating disorders** or severe body image disturbance
- **Persistent anxiety** that prevents the client from engaging in normal life activities
- **The client asks for therapy** — sometimes clients realize they need more than coaching can offer

### How to Refer

Referring a client is not failure. It is professional responsibility.

1. **Name what you are noticing:** "I've noticed that you've been struggling with low energy and lack of motivation for several weeks now. I want to support you, but I think this may be beyond what coaching is designed for."
2. **Normalize the referral:** "Many of my clients have benefited from working with a therapist alongside coaching, or taking a break from coaching to focus on therapeutic work."
3. **Provide resources:** Have a list of therapists, low-cost options, and crisis resources ready.
4. **Stay in your lane:** Do not diagnose. Do not suggest specific diagnoses. You are observing, not clinically assessing.

---

## Summary

Client stuckness is normal and expected. The Stages of Change model helps you assess where the client is and match your approach accordingly. Sometimes you need to change your approach; sometimes you need to sit with the stuckness. Non-completion of worksheets is data about the worksheet, the timing, or the client's readiness—not a character flaw. And when stuckness reflects a clinical issue, the ethical response is to refer to therapy.
