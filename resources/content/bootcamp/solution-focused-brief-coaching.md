## What Is Solution-Focused Brief Coaching?

Solution-Focused Brief Coaching (SFBC) is a goal-directed, future-oriented approach that deliberately avoids analyzing the problem. Developed from Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT) by Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg in the 1980s at the Milwaukee Brief Family Therapy Center, SFBC is built on a radical premise: **you do not need to understand the problem in order to solve it.**

This is counterintuitive. Most traditional problem-solving models assume you must first exhaustively analyze the problem before designing a solution. SFBC says: the more time you spend talking about the problem, the more entrenched it becomes. Instead, focus all energy on describing the solution.

**The SFBC mindset:**
- The client already has the resources they need.
- Small changes lead to bigger changes.
- If it works, do more of it. If it does not work, do something different.
- The solution is not necessarily related to the problem.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> SFBC is not about "positive thinking" or ignoring difficulties. It is a disciplined, structured methodology for helping clients construct a detailed vision of their desired future and identify the concrete steps that are already working. The coach takes the client's difficulties seriously — they just choose to focus the conversation elsewhere.

## The Miracle Question

The Miracle Question is the signature intervention of SFBC. It is designed to bypass the client's problem-saturated thinking and access their imagination of a preferred future.

**The classic phrasing:**

> "I am going to ask you a strange question. Suppose that tonight, while you are asleep, a miracle happens. The miracle is that the problem that brought you here is solved. But because you are asleep, you do not know the miracle happened. When you wake up tomorrow morning, what will be the first small sign that something is different? What will you notice? What will be different?"

### How to Use the Miracle Question

The key is **detailed sensory specificity.** The coach follows up relentlessly:

- "What will you see?"
- "What will you hear?"
- "What will you feel?"
- "What will you be doing differently?"
- "What will your partner/colleague/friend notice about you?"
- "What will they say? What will you say back?"

The more vivid and specific the miracle description, the more it functions as a goal. The client essentially builds a detailed picture of their preferred future — which then becomes the target of coaching.

**Common coaching adaptation:** Some clients find "miracle" too religious or abstract. You can adapt: "If a magic wand solved this problem overnight..." or "If you woke up tomorrow and this was no longer an issue..." or "If I could fast-forward you six months to when this is resolved..."

## Scaling Questions

Scaling questions quantify subjective experience. They convert vague feelings into measurable data that the coach and client can track over time.

**The basic scaling question:**
> "On a scale of 0 to 10, where 10 is the miracle fully realized and 0 is the worst it has ever been, where are you today?"

### Follow-Up Scaling Questions

The real power is in the follow-up:

- **Progress:** "You are at a 4. What tells you it is a 4 and not a 3?"
- **Resources:** "What is already in place that got you to a 4?"
- **Confidence:** "On a scale of 0–10, how confident are you that you can move to a 5?"
- **Next step:** "What would a 5 look like? What is the smallest thing that would move you from a 4 to a 5?"
- **Effort:** "On a scale of 0–10, how much effort are you willing to put into getting to a 5?"

**Why scaling works:** It helps clients notice what is already working (even at a 2, something is working — they are not at 0). It also makes progress tangible and trackable. "Last month you were at a 4; today you are at a 6" is measurable progress that builds momentum.

## Exception-Finding

Exception-finding is the SFBC technique of searching for times when the problem was absent or less severe. Every problem has exceptions — times when it did not happen, or happened less, or was handled differently.

**Exception-finding questions:**
- "Tell me about a time recently when this problem did not happen, even for a short while."
- "When was the last time you felt a little bit better about this?"
- "Are there times when the problem is less intense? What is different about those times?"
- "What was different about that day? What were you doing? Who was there?"

### Turning Exceptions into Solutions

Once the exception is identified, the coach explores how to replicate it:

- "How did you make that happen?"
- "What would it take to do more of that?"
- "On a scale of 0–10, how likely are you to try that again this week?"

The insight: **exceptions are not random — they are evidence of the client's existing resources and successful strategies.** The coach helps the client notice and amplify what they are already doing that works.

> [!WARNING]
> Do not fall into problem analysis when searching for exceptions. The trap: "When was the last time the problem was better? Oh, that was when I was on holiday." Then the client goes into "I need more holidays" which is not a solution. Instead, ask: "What specifically about that holiday context made things better? How can you bring even 10% of that into your daily life?"

## SFBC Session Structure

An SFBC session follows a distinct structure — different from GROW or CBC:

| **Stage** | **Purpose** | **Key Questions** |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Contracting** | Set the focus | "What would you like to get from our session today?" |
| **2. Preferred Future** | Build the miracle | Miracle Question in full. Detailed follow-up. |
| **3. Scaling** | Measure current state | "Where are you on a 0–10 scale relative to the miracle?" |
| **4. Exceptions** | Find what works | "What is already at a 4? What is working?" |
| **5. Small Steps** | Define next action | "What is the smallest thing that would move you one point up the scale?" |
| **6. Session Summary** | Reinforce progress | "What have you learned today? What will you do?" |

## SFBC in Practice: What to Avoid

| **Common Mistake** | **Why It Fails** | **SFBC Alternative** |
|---|---|---|
| Spending 20 minutes on the problem | Clients feel worse, problem grows | Move to the Miracle Question within 5 minutes |
| Asking "why" questions | Encourages problem analysis | Ask "what" and "how" questions |
| Telling the client what to do | Undermines client autonomy | "What do you think would work?" |
| Setting big, vague goals | Hard to track progress | Small, specific, behavioral steps |
| Searching for root causes | not relevant in SFBC | Search for exceptions |

## When to Use SFBC vs. GROW

SFBC is particularly effective when:
- The client is stuck in problem-talk and keeps circling back to difficulties.
- The client has been "analyzing" without progress for a long time.
- The client responds well to forward-focused, positive framing.
- You have limited sessions (SFBC is inherently brief — often 3–6 sessions).

GROW is better when:
- The client has a clear, concrete goal and wants a structured path.
- The client needs to work through complex decision-making.
- The Reality stage provides useful data (financial planning, career strategy).
