## The Purpose of the First Session

The first session is not where coaching *happens* -- it is where coaching becomes *possible*. Its primary job is to build enough trust, clarity, and mutual understanding that the real work can begin in session two.

A well-run intake and discovery session accomplishes four things:

1. **Builds trust and safety.** The client needs to feel heard, seen, and safe before they will risk revealing the uncomfortable truths that drive real change.
2. **Understands the client's world.** You cannot coach what you do not understand. The discovery phase maps the client's current reality, past efforts, values, and desired future.
3. **Sets expectations.** Coaching fails when the client expects advice and the coach expects introspection. Agreement on what coaching is -- and is not -- prevents misalignment.
4. **Establishes the coaching agreement.** Whether verbal or written, the agreement defines scope (what you will work on), logistics (frequency, duration, fees), and boundaries (confidentiality, cancellations, referral thresholds).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The discovery session is not therapy intake, not consulting diagnosis, and not a friendly chat. It is a **professional contracting conversation** with a relational foundation. Move too fast toward contracting and you lose trust. Never establish contracting and you lose structure.

## Pre-Session Preparation

The work of the first session begins before the client joins the call. Sending an intake worksheet 48--72 hours in advance gives the client time to reflect and gives you a data-rich starting point.

### The Intake Worksheet Packet

Your intake packet should include at least three elements:

**1. Wheel of Life assessment.**
Ask the client to rate their satisfaction (1--10) across 6--10 life domains: career, health, relationships, personal growth, finances, fun, physical environment, and spirituality/spiritual well-being. This immediately reveals where the client senses the biggest gap and gives you a visual starting point for the conversation.

Link: [Wheel of Life worksheet template](https://coachsheets.app/library/worksheets)

**2. Values assessment.**
A brief values inventory (e.g., "pick your top 5 from this list of 40 values") surfaces what matters most to the client. This is invaluable for session two, where you can begin aligning goals with values.

**3. Goals inventory.**
Three simple prompts:
- "What specific outcome do you most want from coaching?"
- "What would success look like in 6 months?"
- "What have you already tried on your own?"

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Do not skip the pre-session worksheet. Coaches who skip it spend the first 30 minutes of the discovery call gathering information that could have been gathered before the call. That is wasted billable time and wasted relational momentum.

## The Discovery Call Structure (60--90 Minutes)

The following six-phase structure creates a predictable container that balances rapport, information gathering, and forward movement.

### 1. Welcome & Rapport Building (5 minutes)

Purpose: Transition the client from their day into the coaching space.

- Begin with a genuine check-in: "How is your day? What is it like arriving here?"
- Name what you notice: "I'm glad you made time for this."
- Briefly orient them to the call structure: "Here is how I'd like to spend our time today."
- Confirm timing: "We have about 75 minutes. Does that still work for you?"

**Do not** spend 15 minutes on small talk. Five minutes is enough. The client is here to work, and extended rapport building can actually *reduce* trust by making the client wonder if you know what you are doing.

### 2. Coaching Agreement & Scope Clarification (10 minutes)

This phase is the most skipped and the most important. State explicitly what coaching is and is not, and get the client's verbal agreement.

Suggested language:
> "I want to be clear about what coaching is and isn't. Coaching is a partnership where I listen deeply, ask questions, and help you clarify your own thinking. I do not give advice, diagnose, or treat mental health conditions. If at any point it sounds like something beyond coaching is needed -- like therapy or medical advice -- I will say so, and I may refer you to someone else. Does that feel right to you? Do you have any questions about how this works?"

Cover these points:
- Coaching is not therapy (different purpose, different training, different scope)
- The client sets the agenda; the coach facilitates
- Confidentiality terms and its limits
- Logistics: session frequency, duration, fees, cancellation policy
- What happens between sessions (reflection, experiments, no "homework" unless client commits)

This is also the moment to align with the [ICF Core Competency #2: Embodies a Coaching Mindset](https://coachingfederation.org/core-competencies) and Competency #3: Establishes and Maintains Agreements.

### 3. Client's Story & Current Situation (15--20 minutes)

Purpose: Understand where the client is *right now*.

Use the intake worksheet as your map. Do not ask every question on the worksheet aloud -- you already have the answers. Instead, say: "I read through your intake. I want to focus on what stood out to you as you filled it out."

Key prompts:
- "As you look at your Wheel of Life, what jumps out?"
- "Tell me about the area with the lowest score. What is happening there?"
- "What else is going on that the form did not capture?"
- "Who else is affected by this situation?"

> [!IMPORTANT]
> At this stage, you are listening for **patterns, not problems**. The client will hand you dozens of problems. Your job is to notice which ones are connected. The real coaching topic is rarely the first thing the client mentions.

### 4. Goals & Desired Outcomes (15 minutes)

Purpose: Define what the client wants *instead*.

Transition: "I have a good picture of where you are. Now tell me: where do you want to be?"

Key prompts:
- "What would success look like in 6 months? Paint me a picture."
- "How will you know when you have achieved it? What will be different?"
- "What will it cost you -- emotionally, relationally, practically -- if nothing changes?"
- "Who do you need to become to make that future happen?"

Guide the client toward **SMART** outcomes, but do not force it. The first session goal is direction, not precision. You will sharpen the goal in later sessions.

### 5. Values & Motivations Exploration (10 minutes)

Purpose: Connect goals to what matters most.

Refer back to the values inventory the client completed in the intake worksheet.

Key prompts:
- "You said [value] is one of your top values. How is your current situation honoring or violating that value?"
- "What is the deeper 'why' behind this goal?"
- "What does achieving this goal say about who you are as a person?"

This phase is the bridge from the discovery call to session two, where you will use values to test and strengthen the client's goals.

### 6. Next Steps & Between-Session Assignment (5 minutes)

Purpose: End with clarity and momentum.

- Summarize what you heard: "Here is what I am hearing is most important to you..."
- Ask the client to confirm or correct: "Does that sound right? What did I miss?"
- Propose a focus area for the next session: "I suggest we start next session by exploring [topic] in more depth. How does that sound?"
- Give a light between-session reflection: "Between now and our next session, notice when [pattern] shows up. Do not try to change it -- just notice it. Bring your observations."

Schedule the next session before the call ends.

## Key Questions to Ask in Discovery

These seven questions consistently surface the most useful information:

1. **"What brings you to coaching now?"** -- reveals the trigger event or tipping point.
2. **"What would success look like in 6 months?"** -- surfaces outcome clarity (or lack of it).
3. **"What have you already tried?"** -- reveals resourcefulness, past failures, and beliefs about what works.
4. **"What do you know about how you get in your own way?"** -- uncovers the client's self-awareness and their theory of their own problem.
5. **"Who else is affected by this situation?"** -- reveals the relational stakes.
6. **"What is the cost of staying where you are?"** -- surfaces motivation and urgency.
7. **"What do you most want me to understand about you?"** -- invites the client to name what they need you to know.

## Common Mistakes in the First Session

### Mistake 1: Talking Too Much
New coaches over-explain coaching, share personal stories, and fill silences. **Silence is a coaching tool.** After you ask a question, wait. The client is thinking. Interrupting that thinking is stealing their insight.

### Mistake 2: Rushing to Solutions
The client hands you a problem and you want to help. So you jump to frameworks, tools, and action plans. Resist this. **The first session is for discovery and understanding, not rushing into solutions.** You cannot support a meaningful pathway for a goal or obstacle you do not yet fully understand.

### Mistake 3: Skipping the Agreement
Coaches assume the client already knows what coaching is. They do not. Without explicit scope and boundary setting, the client may expect therapy, consulting, or a friendly venting session. **Always state the agreement, even with returning clients.**

### Mistake 4: Not Clarifying Scope
Discovery reveals more problems than you can solve in a coaching engagement. Trying to work on everything at once is a recipe for nothing getting done. **Help the client pick one to three focus areas for the engagement.** The rest can wait.

### Mistake 5: Forgetting to Leave Time for Next Steps
A discovery call that ends abruptly -- "Well, great talking, let's do this again" -- leaves the client unsure of what just happened. **Always leave 5 minutes for summary, confirmation, and next steps.** The client should leave the call knowing exactly what comes next.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> These five mistakes are not minor. Research from the ICF and coaching psychology literature shows that unclear agreements and premature solutioning are among the top reasons coaching engagements fail before they truly begin.
