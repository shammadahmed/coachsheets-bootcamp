# Module 4, Lesson 1: Pre-Session Preparation

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Design a repeatable pre-session preparation routine that takes 10-15 minutes per client
- Review client worksheets, submissions, and AI summaries to identify themes and patterns
- Craft a tentative session agenda while maintaining the flexibility to follow what emerges
- Prepare 2-3 powerful, open-ended questions rooted in the client's recent work

---

## Why Pre-Session Preparation Matters

The quality of a coaching session is often determined before the coach and client ever connect. Preparation is the unsung hero of great coaching. Without it, sessions tend to meander, revisit old ground, or fail to build on the momentum created in previous conversations.

Great coaches do not "wing it." They walk into a session with a clear sense of what happened in the client's world since they last spoke, what themes are surfacing, and where the edge of the client's growth might be found. This does not mean scripting the session or rigidly planning every question. It means arriving present, informed, and ready to meet the client wherever they are.

A 2023 study published in the *International Journal of Evidence Based Coaching and Mentoring* found that coach preparation was significantly correlated with client outcomes, particularly around goal attainment and self-awareness. Coaches who reported spending at least 10 minutes preparing per session had clients who reported higher satisfaction and clearer progress. This is not optional—it is part of the professional standard.

> "The best improvisers are the best preparers. They know their material so well they can afford to let it go." — Patricia Ryan Madson, *Improv Wisdom*

## The Preparation Workflow

A repeatable preparation routine ensures consistency across all your clients. Here is a four-step workflow designed to fit into 10-15 minutes per client.

### Step 1: Review Client Worksheets and Submissions (5 minutes)

Start with the client's submitted worksheet from the past week (or since your last session). Read it first for content, then read it again for subtext. Ask yourself:

- **What did the client choose to write about?** The topics a client selects are as important as what they say about them.
- **Where did the client go deep?** Look for answers that are unusually detailed, emotional, or reflective. These signal areas of high engagement.
- **Where did the client skim?** Brief or superficial answers may indicate resistance, confusion, or simply a busy week. Note these but do not assume.
- **What is missing?** If the worksheet asked about progress on a specific goal and the client did not mention it, that absence is data.

### Step 2: Review AI Summaries for Emerging Themes (3 minutes)

If you use CoachSheets' AI summary feature, read the generated recap before re-reading the full submission. The AI summary will flag:

- **Recurring themes** — topics that have appeared across multiple submissions or sessions
- **Shifts in perspective or energy** — changes from previous sessions worth exploring
- **Potential red flags** — language that may indicate distress, avoidance, or concern
- **Contradictions** — statements that conflict with previous goals or stated intentions

Treat the AI summary as a *first pass*, not a final analysis. Always verify its observations against your own reading of the original submission. AI is a tool to amplify your attention, not replace it.

### Step 3: Set a Tentative Agenda (3 minutes)

Based on your review, write down a tentative session agenda. Keep it to three items or fewer:

1. A brief check-in anchor (e.g., "I noticed in your worksheet you mentioned feeling stuck around X — can we start there?")
2. A deep exploration topic (the main area of growth or challenge)
3. An intention for the session outcome (what you hope the client walks away with)

Write this agenda in your coaching notes or in CoachSheets' session preparation field. But here is the critical caveat: **hold it loosely**. Sessions unfold in unpredictable ways. The agenda is a compass, not a GPS.

### Step 4: Prepare 2-3 Powerful Questions (2 minutes)

Based on your review and tentative agenda, craft 2-3 powerful questions. Powerful coaching questions share common qualities:

- **They are open-ended.** Avoid questions that can be answered with yes, no, or a single fact. "What was that like for you?" rather than "Was that hard?"
- **They emerge from what you have read.** The best questions show the client you have truly engaged with their work. "You wrote that you felt 'flat' after the presentation — help me understand what 'flat' means for you."
- **They aim forward.** Even when exploring the past, great questions connect to future action. "Given what you are noticing about your procrastination, what is one small experiment you could run this week?"

## Common Preparation Pitfalls

### Over-Preparation

It is possible to prepare too much. If you arrive at a session with a detailed script and a fixed outcome in mind, you will be unable to follow the client when they take the conversation somewhere unexpected. The client always knows their own terrain better than you do. Your preparation should make you more flexible, not less.

### Under-Preparation

The more dangerous sin for most new coaches. Arriving without having read the client's submission wastes the client's time and signals that you do not value their work enough to engage with it. If you are short on time, prioritize reading the client's submission over everything else. A single powerful question rooted in their words is worth more than a perfectly structured agenda.

### Confirmation Bias

When reviewing client submissions, it is easy to see only what confirms your existing hypotheses about the client. Guard against this by actively looking for evidence that contradicts your assumptions. If you believe a client is "resistant," look for moments of genuine engagement. If you believe a client is "making progress," look for signs of avoidance or surface-level compliance.

## Exercise: Session Prep Practice

Read the following sample client submission and write 200-300 words of session preparation notes.

**Client context:** Sarah, 34, mid-level manager. She has been working with you for six weeks on "feeling more confident in leadership." She completed a Thought Record worksheet.

**Sample worksheet response:**

"Situation: I had to present our quarterly results to the senior leadership team. I prepared extensively but the night before I couldn't sleep. During the presentation, I felt my voice shake and I rushed through the last three slides. Afterward, my boss said 'good job' but I felt like I'd failed.

Automatic thought: 'They can all see I'm not qualified for this role.'

Evidence for: I stumbled on the Q3 numbers. I forgot to mention the marketing campaign results.

Evidence against: My boss specifically asked me to present. The VP said the analysis was thorough. I've been in this role for two years and have never received negative feedback on my work.

Alternative thought: 'I may not have been perfect, but I am qualified. My anxiety about performing well shows I care about doing a good job.'

How I feel now: A little better after writing this, but I still have a pit in my stomach. Part of me doesn't believe the alternative thought."

**Your task:** Write preparation notes addressing:

1. What themes or patterns do you notice?
2. What is your tentative agenda?
3. What 2-3 powerful questions will you bring?
4. What do you want to hold loosely going in?

## Summary

Pre-session preparation is the foundation of effective coaching. A consistent preparation routine—reviewing submissions, analyzing AI summaries, setting a tentative agenda, and crafting powerful questions—ensures you show up fully present and informed. The goal is not to control the session but to be ready for wherever it goes.
