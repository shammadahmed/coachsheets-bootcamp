# Lesson 3.2: Worksheet Design Principles

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Identify the characteristics of an effective coaching worksheet
- Match question types to coaching objectives (wheel for assessment, scale for intensity, cards for sorting, textarea for reflection)
- Apply progressive disclosure to structure worksheet flow
- Recognize and avoid common worksheet design mistakes
- Explain mobile-first design considerations for client worksheets

---

## What Makes a Good Coaching Worksheet?

A coaching worksheet is not a quiz, a survey, or a homework assignment. It is a **structured reflection tool** designed to help clients achieve insights between sessions. The best worksheets feel like a good coaching conversation translated into written form.

### Clear Instructions

Every question must answer three unstated client concerns: "What am I being asked to do?", "Why does this matter?", and "How much time should I spend on this?" A question like "Describe your relationship with time" is too vague. Instead, try: "Think about your typical workday. Where do you feel most rushed? Where do you feel most in flow? Write for about 3 minutes (aim for one paragraph)."

The instruction sets the stage for the quality of response. Vague instructions produce shallow answers. Specific instructions produce reflective, useful answers. The National Literacy Trust provides research on how instructions affect task completion, relevant here: https://literacytrust.org.uk/policy-and-campaigns/all-party-parliamentary-group-literacy/lost-for-words/.

### One Focus Per Question

Each question should explore exactly one dimension. A question that asks "How satisfied are you with your career and your relationships?" forces the client to collapse two distinct domains into a single answer. Split it: "How satisfied are you with your career right now?" followed by "How satisfied are you with your key relationships?"

This principle applies across element types. A Wheel of Life works because it separates life into distinct domains and measures each independently. If you cram multiple concepts into one wheel segment, the data loses meaning.

### Matching Element Type to Objective

The CoachSheets platform offers over 40 element types. Choosing the right one is a design decision that directly affects the quality of client reflection.

| Coaching Objective | Best Element Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Assess current state across domains | Wheel of Life | Visual, holistic, instantly reveals gaps |
| Measure intensity of a feeling or priority | Scale / Slider | Precise, trackable over time |
| Sort priorities or values | Cards (Card Sort) | Forces ranking, reveals trade-offs |
| Surface unconscious beliefs | Thought Record / Cognitive Distortions | Structured reflection with cognitive framework |
| Explore identity and habits | Habit Identity / Vision Board | Creative, aspirational, future-focused |
| Free reflection and journaling | Textarea | Open-ended, no constraints |
| Make a decision | Decision Matrix / Eisenhower Matrix | Structured comparison, action-oriented |
| Track progress over time | Progress Rings / Confidence Ladder | Visual, motivating, quantitative |

The wrong element type undermines the coaching goal. For example, using a textarea for values clarification asks the client to list their values from memory. Using a Card Sort element forces them to choose, rank, and trade off -- a far richer exercise. The theoretical foundation for this matching comes from assessment design research: https://www.cambridge.org/assessment/.

### Mobile-First Design

Over 70% of coaching clients complete worksheets on their phones. If your worksheet does not work well on mobile, most clients will abandon it.

Mobile-first worksheet design means:
- **Short scrollable sections** rather than long pages. A question that requires a 400-word textarea might feel fine on desktop but overwhelming on a phone.
- **Large touch targets** for interactive elements. Card sorting requires cards big enough to tap and drag on a 6-inch screen.
- **Single-column layout**. Never put two interactive elements side by side on mobile.
- **Auto-save progress**. Mobile users frequently switch apps, take calls, or get interrupted. Auto-save means they never lose work.
- **Minimal typing**. Prefer sliders, scales, and card sorts over text entry when possible. Typing on a phone is friction.

Google's research on mobile form optimization is directly applicable to worksheets: https://developers.google.com/web/fundamentals/design-and-ux/input/forms.

### Progressive Disclosure

Worksheets should follow a logical emotional arc. The easiest, most approachable questions come first. Deeper, more vulnerable questions come later. This is progressive disclosure.

A well-structured worksheet might flow like this:
1. **Check-in question** (easy): "On a scale of 1-10, how is your energy today?" -- Establishes a low-friction entry point.
2. **Assessment question** (moderate): Complete the Wheel of Life. -- Provides structure and visual engagement.
3. **Reflection question** (deeper): "Looking at your Wheel, which area surprised you most?" -- Builds on the previous answer.
4. **Vulnerable question** (deepest): "What is the story you tell yourself about why this area is stuck?" -- Requires trust and emotional safety, earned by the preceding questions.
5. **Forward-looking question** (action-oriented): "What is one small step you could take this week?" -- Ends on an empowering note.

This structure respects the client's emotional journey through the worksheet. It mirrors how a skilled coach would sequence questions in a live session. The UX principles behind progressive disclosure are explained at https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/.

## Common Worksheet Design Mistakes

### Too Many Questions

The fastest way to kill completion rates is to ask too many questions. A worksheet should take 10-15 minutes to complete. For an intake worksheet, 20 minutes maximum. Beyond that, clients rush, skip, or abandon.

A good rule of thumb: no more than 8-10 questions for a standard between-session worksheet, and no more than 12-15 for an intake. Every question must earn its place. If you cannot articulate why a specific question is essential, remove it.

### Vague Prompts

"Tell me about your goals" is a prompt, not a coaching question. The client does not know where to start. Better: "Pick one goal that matters most to you right now. Write one paragraph describing what achieving it would look like and feel like. What would be different in your daily life?"

Specificity is kind to the client. It reduces the cognitive load of "what should I write?" and channels their energy into the reflection itself.

### No Context or Purpose

Clients complete worksheets more thoughtfully when they understand why. A brief introduction at the top of each worksheet sets the frame: "This worksheet helps us identify your core values so we can align your goals with what truly matters to you. It will take about 12 minutes."

Without context, a worksheet feels like busywork. With context, it feels like a meaningful part of the coaching journey.

### Ignoring the Post-Completion Experience

What happens after the client submits the worksheet? Ideally, they feel a sense of accomplishment and curiosity about what the coach will say. A confirmation message matters: "Great work! Your coach will review this before your next session. In the meantime, notice any patterns that came up for you."

Never let the worksheet end in a void. The completion moment is an opportunity to reinforce the coaching relationship.
