# Lesson 6.2: Coaching Through Transitions — The Psychology of Change

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- Distinguish between change (external event) and transition (internal psychological process)
- Apply William Bridges' three-phase transition model in coaching sessions
- Coach clients through common life transitions including career change, divorce, retirement, relocation, and loss
- Recognise the boundary between coaching through grief and referring to therapy
- Design coaching interventions appropriate to each transition phase

---

## Change vs. Transition

A common mistake new coaches make is treating transition coaching like project management. They help the client plan the logistics of a move, a career shift, or a divorce without attending to the internal psychological journey. William Bridges, the seminal thinker on organisational and personal transition, drew a critical distinction:

**Change is situational.** It is the external event: the new job, the empty nest, the relocation, the divorce filing.

**Transition is psychological.** It is the internal reorientation and redefinition that people go through to come to terms with the new situation. A person can make the external change (show up at the new job, move into the new house) without ever making the internal transition. That is when clients feel stuck, disoriented, or hollow.

> "It isn't the changes that do you in, it's the transitions." — William Bridges

---

## The Three Phases of Transition

Bridges identified three sequential phases. Clients move through them at different paces and may oscillate between phases. The coach's role shifts depending on which phase the client is in.

### Phase 1: Ending, Losing, Letting Go

Every transition begins with an ending. Even positive changes (a promotion, a wedding, a new baby) involve losing something — a previous identity, familiar routines, old relationships, a sense of competence.

Common experiences in this phase:
- Disorientation and confusion
- Denial or minimisation of the loss
- Anger, sadness, or numbness
- Physical symptoms (fatigue, disrupted sleep)
- A sense of disintegration or falling apart

**Coaching interventions:**
- Normalise the experience. "What you are feeling is a natural part of transition. You are not broken."
- Help the client name what is ending. Create a "loss list" — everything they are leaving behind.
- Honour what was good. Rituals can be powerful: a farewell letter, a small ceremony, a gratitude practice for what is ending.
- Resist the urge to rush toward solutions. Premature positivity can prevent the client from processing the ending.

### Phase 2: The Neutral Zone

This is the most challenging and most creative phase. The old is gone, but the new has not yet taken shape. Clients describe feeling in limbo, between worlds, unanchored.

Common experiences:
- Anxiety and uncertainty
- Creativity and experimentation
- Renewed energy punctuated by despair
- Questioning of fundamental identity
- Heightened sensitivity and intuition

**Coaching interventions:**
- Help the client build temporary structures. Routines, daily anchors, and small commitments provide stability without locking the client into a permanent direction.
- Encourage experimentation. The neutral zone is ideal for trying on identities, exploring options, and gathering data without commitment.
- Use journaling and reflection. This is a fertile period for insight if the client can tolerate the uncertainty.
- Reframe the neutral zone. "You are not stuck. You are in the chrysalis phase. Something is forming."
- Manage the client's support system. Help them communicate to family and friends that they are in a transition and need patience.

### Phase 3: The New Beginning

The new beginning is not a return to the old normal. It is an emergence into a new identity and a new way of being. Bridges cautions that beginnings cannot be forced. They emerge naturally when the work of the neutral zone is done.

Signs the client is entering this phase:
- Renewed energy and purpose
- Clearer sense of direction
- Willingness to make commitments
- Integration of the ending's lessons
- A felt sense of "coming home" to oneself

**Coaching interventions:**
- Help the client articulate what they have learned from the journey. What did the ending teach them? What emerged in the neutral zone?
- Support commitment to the new direction. Translate insights into concrete plans.
- Identify the inner signals that tell the client they are aligned with their new beginning.
- Celebrate. Transition is hard work, and acknowledgment matters.

## Common Life Transitions and Coaching Approaches

| Transition Type | Key Coaching Considerations |
|---|---|
| **Career change** | Identity often tied to profession. Explore values and strengths that may have been suppressed. Address fear of starting over. |
| **Divorce/separation** | Grief for the relationship and the shared future. Rebuilding identity as an individual. Practical logistics may need referral to other professionals. |
| **Retirement** | Loss of professional identity, social structure, and sense of purpose. Re-defining "retirement" beyond not working. |
| **Relocation** | Loss of community, familiar environment, and autonomy. Cultural adjustment if moving internationally. Building new social structures. |
| **Loss of a loved one** | Grief work. **Boundary alert:** persistent complicated grief may require therapy referral. Coaching focuses on rebuilding life while honouring the loss. |
| **Empty nest** | Shift in parenting identity. Reclaiming space and time. Rediscovering the couple relationship or individual pursuits. |

## The Grief and Loss Boundary

This is one of the most important boundaries in transition coaching. Grief is a normal human response to loss, and coaches absolutely work with clients who are grieving. However, there is a distinction between:

- **Normal grief** — Sadness, longing, difficulty concentrating, oscillating between acceptance and pain. This is within the coaching scope, provided the coach is not diagnosing or treating.
- **Complicated grief / Prolonged grief disorder** — Persistent, intense grief that does not improve over time (typically more than 12 months), involving identity disruption, disbelief, avoidance of reminders, and significant functional impairment. This requires referral to a therapist.

Signs to refer:
- The client cannot function in daily life for extended periods
- The client expresses suicidal ideation (engage crisis resources immediately)
- The client is using substances to cope to a dangerous degree
- Grief symptoms are worsening rather than stabilising over time

You can coach alongside therapy. Many grieving clients benefit from both a therapist (for processing the loss) and a coach (for rebuilding life). Maintain communication with the client about how both relationships serve them.
