## The Onboarding Flow

A professional onboarding experience sets the tone for the entire coaching relationship. When a client signs up, their first impression of your process will either build trust or create doubt. A well-designed onboarding flow has four stages:

### Stage 1: Discovery Call

This is not a sales pitch; it is a mutual exploration. The discovery call (usually 20-30 minutes, free of charge) allows you to:

- Understand the client's current situation and desired outcome
- Assess whether your niche and approach are a good fit
- Explain how your coaching package works and what it includes
- Answer questions about logistics, commitment, and expectations
- Decide together whether to move forward

Not every discovery call should lead to a signed client. Turning away clients who are not a good fit protects your energy and your reputation.

### Stage 2: Coaching Agreement

Once a client decides to proceed, they receive your coaching agreement (contract). This should be sent digitally for e-signature. CoachSheets does not handle contracts directly, but you can use tools like HelloSign, PandaDoc, or even a simple PDF sent via email. Key sections include:

- Scope of services
- Fees and payment terms
- Session logistics (duration, frequency, platform)
- Cancellation and rescheduling policy
- Confidentiality and data protection
- Mutual expectations and responsibilities
- Termination clause

Never start coaching without a signed agreement. It protects both you and the client.

### Stage 3: Intake Worksheet

With the agreement signed, send the client your intake worksheet. This is where CoachSheets shines. Using the magic link feature:

1. Create a worksheet with intake questions (goals, challenges, history, baseline metrics).
2. Set `send_at` to trigger immediately upon enrollment.
3. The client receives an email with a unique magic link.
4. They complete the worksheet on any device without needing an account.

You can also set up an automation rule in CoachSheets that triggers when a client is created, automatically sending the intake worksheet. This makes onboarding nearly hands-off.

Include questions like:

- What brings you to coaching at this time?
- What specific outcomes do you want to achieve?
- On a scale of 1-10, where are you now relative to your goal?
- What have you tried before, and what worked or did not work?
- What is your availability for sessions?
- Is there anything you want me to know about your background or circumstances?

### Stage 4: First Session

Before the first session, review the completed intake worksheet thoroughly. Your first session should:

- Establish rapport and safety
- Reflect back what you heard in the intake
- Clarify the coaching container (how you work, what to expect)
- Set a specific goal or intention for the engagement
- End with a clear action step

A strong first session sets the trajectory for the entire engagement. Do not rush it.

## Using CoachSheets for Onboarding

CoachSheets provides several features that make onboarding efficient and professional:

### Magic Links

The magic link feature (`/fill/{token}`) allows clients to complete worksheets without creating an account. Use this for:

- Intake worksheets before the first session
- Between-session reflection worksheets
- Feedback forms at the end of an engagement

No passwords, no login, no friction.

### Automation Rules

Create an automation rule in CoachSheets that:

- **Trigger**: When a new client is created
- **Action**: Send the intake worksheet immediately
- **Timing**: At creation time (not delayed)

This ensures every new client receives their intake worksheet without you remembering to send it manually.

### Program Enrollments

If you offer multi-session programs with structured worksheets, use CoachSheets' program enrollment feature. Define a program with steps, each step having an associated worksheet and a scheduled send time. When a client enrolls, the program automatically delivers worksheets on the schedule you define.

## Contracts: What to Include

Your coaching agreement protects both parties and sets clear expectations. While you should have a lawyer review your final contract, here are the essential sections:

1. **Parties and Effective Date**: Who is involved and when the agreement starts.

2. **Scope of Services**: What you will and will not do. Be explicit. If you do not provide therapy, financial advice, or legal counsel, say so. Sample language:
   > "Coaching is not therapy, counselling, or medical advice. The client understands that coaching addresses goals and accountability, not mental health treatment."

3. **Fees and Payment**: Total package price, payment schedule (upfront, monthly instalments), accepted payment methods, late payment consequences.

4. **Session Logistics**: Session length (e.g., 50 minutes), frequency, platform (Zoom, phone, in-person), how sessions are scheduled.

5. **Cancellation Policy**: How much notice is required (typically 24-48 hours), what happens to missed sessions, whether sessions can be rescheduled.

6. **Confidentiality**: What information is kept confidential, any exceptions (harm to self or others, legal requirements).

7. **Termination**: How either party can end the agreement, refund policy (if any) for unused sessions.

8. **Intellectual Property**: Who owns the worksheets, tools, and materials provided during coaching.

9. **Signature**: Both parties sign and date.

## Drafting a Coaching Package Proposal

Before you can onboard clients, you need a clear package proposal. This is a one-page document that describes what you offer. It serves as the bridge between the discovery call and the formal agreement.

Your package proposal should include:

- **Package name**: Something memorable that communicates the transformation.
- **Target client**: One sentence describing who this is for.
- **Outcome**: What the client will achieve by the end.
- **What is included**: Number of sessions, duration, worksheets, support.
- **Investment**: The total price and payment options.
- **Your bio**: 2-3 sentences establishing your credibility.

Keep the proposal to one page. It is a decision-making tool, not a novel.
