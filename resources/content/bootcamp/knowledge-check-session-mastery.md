# Module 4, Lesson 7: Knowledge Check — Session Mastery

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Apply the principles from Module 4 (preparation, session structure, notes, AI summaries, progress tracking, stuckness) to realistic coaching scenarios
- Make sound professional judgments in ambiguous coaching situations
- Identify when a coaching approach needs adjustment and when to persevere

---

## Instructions

This knowledge check contains 12 scenario-based questions designed to test your understanding of Session Mastery concepts. Read each scenario carefully and write a response of 2-4 paragraphs addressing the question. Answers are provided after the questions for self-assessment.

---

## Questions

### Question 1: The Missing Worksheets

A client has missed their last three worksheet submissions. They have been in coaching with you for 3 months. Initially, they completed worksheets reliably. When you asked about it last session, they said they were "just busy." Their session attendance remains consistent.

**What do you do?** Consider the possible explanations and outline your approach.

### Question 2: The AI Red Flag

You read an AI summary of a client's worksheet that flags a potential red flag: "Client uses language suggesting increased alcohol consumption ('a few drinks to take the edge off,' 'wine helps me unwind')." This is the first time this language has appeared in 10 sessions.

**What is your response?** Walk through your verification process and your approach to the next session.

### Question 3: The Overwhelmed Agenda-Setter

You are 20 minutes into a 45-minute session. You set a collaborative agenda in the opening, deciding to work on the client's career transition. However, the client keeps circling back to a conflict with their teenage child. The career topic was their idea, but the family conflict seems to carry more emotional charge.

**Do you hold the agenda or follow the emergent thread?** Explain your reasoning and what you would say.

### Question 4: The Plateau

A client has been working with you for 5 months on building leadership presence. Early sessions showed clear progress (leading indicators improved, client reported feeling more confident). For the last 6 sessions, nothing seems to be shifting. The client says they are "still working on it," but their worksheets and session content feel repetitive.

**How do you assess whether this is a natural plateau or a sign that the coaching approach needs to change?** What specific steps do you take?

### Question 5: The Know-It-All

A new client comes to coaching with a clear sense of what they need. They have read multiple self-help books, have a framework for understanding their challenges, and frequently correct or redirect your questions. They complete worksheets thoroughly but seem to treat coaching as a checkbox rather than a genuine exploration.

**How do you work with a client who seems intellectually engaged but emotionally distant from the coaching process?** How do you address surface compliance?

### Question 6: The Client Who Wants Therapy

A client you have worked with for 4 sessions begins sharing detailed experiences of childhood emotional neglect. They become tearful during the session and say, "I've never told anyone this before." They ask: "Is this something coaching can help with?"

**What do you say?** How do you assess whether this is within coaching scope? What is your referral protocol?

### Question 7: The Time Runaway

You are 35 minutes into a 45-minute session. The client is in the middle of a powerful exploration—they just connected a pattern of people-pleasing to a childhood experience. The energy in the room is high. You have not yet moved to the action phase.

**Do you interrupt the exploration to move toward closure, or do you let the session run over?** What do you say and why?

### Question 8: The Inconsistent Completer

A client completes about 50% of assigned worksheets. When they complete them, the depth is excellent and the sessions are productive. When they do not complete them, the sessions feel shallow and the client seems distracted. The pattern has held for 8 weeks.

**How do you address this pattern?** Is 50% completion acceptable if the sessions are productive when they complete? Outline your approach.

### Question 9: The Contradiction

Your AI summary flags a contradiction: "Client states work-life balance is a top priority but describes working 60-hour weeks and responding to emails at 11pm." This is the third session in a row this contradiction has appeared.

**How do you approach this with the client?** What would you say? Would you use the AI summary in the session?

### Question 10: The Reluctant Participant

A client was referred to coaching by their employer as part of a leadership development program. They did not seek coaching on their own. In the first session, they were polite but clearly disengaged. When you asked about their goals, they said, "My boss thought this would be helpful."

**What stage of change is this client likely in?** What is your coaching approach for the first 2-3 sessions? How do you build engagement?

### Question 11: The Session Notes Audit

You review your last 10 sessions of notes for a particular client and notice that every single session note includes the phrase "client explored [topic]." The topics are all different—career, relationships, health, purpose—and there is no continuity between sessions. No action items are recorded after the first 3 sessions.

**What does this pattern tell you about your coaching with this client?** What changes would you make to the coaching structure?

### Question 12: The Breakthrough

A client who has been stuck on the same issue for 8 weeks has a genuine breakthrough in a session. They connect a long-standing pattern of self-sabotage to a core belief formed in adolescence. The client is visibly moved and says they feel "like a weight has lifted."

**How do you close a session that has had a breakthrough?** What do you do differently in the closing phase? What kind of between-session work is appropriate after a breakthrough?

---

## Answers

### Answer 1: The Missing Worksheets

Non-completion after a period of consistent completion is a change worth exploring. Start with curiosity, not confrontation. Possible explanations include: the worksheets have become too easy or too hard; the client is avoiding a specific topic that has surfaced; external stressors have increased; or the client's engagement is waning.

Approach: In the next session, open with a genuine question: "I noticed the worksheets have been harder to complete lately. What's been happening?" Avoid making the client defensive. Listen to their explanation without jumping to solutions. Consider offering a different type of worksheet or reducing the frequency. Also, ask directly whether the coaching focus still feels right. Sometimes non-completion signals that the coaching goals need recalibrating.

### Answer 2: The AI Red Flag

First, verify against the original submission. Read the exact language the client used. Consider context: did they mention alcohol casually or with concern? Is there cultural context that might change the interpretation? Note that this is the first appearance of this language in 10 sessions.

Approach: In the session, name what you noticed without alarm. "I noticed in your worksheet you mentioned using wine to unwind a couple of times. I'm curious how that's been for you." Stay neutral and observational. Do not confront or diagnose. If the client confirms increased drinking, explore the context: What changed? How do they feel about it? Assess whether it is situational (stressful period) or a pattern that may need clinical support. If you suspect it is moving toward problematic use, refer to a therapist or addiction specialist. Follow your safeguarding protocol.

### Answer 3: The Overwhelmed Agenda-Setter

Follow the emergent thread. The family conflict carries more emotional charge, which means it is where the energy and potential for shift exist. The career topic can wait.

How to handle it: "I notice the situation with your child is coming up with a lot of energy. We set out to talk about the career transition, but I wonder if there's something important here for us to explore. What feels most alive for you right now?" This honors the collaborative agenda while inviting the client to reprioritize. If the client confirms the family topic is more pressing, redirect the session. You can return to the career topic next session.

### Answer 4: The Plateau

Distinguish between a natural plateau (consolidation phase) and a need for approach change. Check leading indicators: Are worksheets still being completed with depth? Is the client showing up engaged? Review 6 sessions for any subtle shifts you may have missed.

Steps to take: (1) Name the plateau directly with the client: "I've noticed we've been working on leadership presence for a while, and the conversation feels like it's looping. What's your take on where things are?" (2) Try a different assessment tool or worksheet type to get fresh data. (3) Consider whether the goal itself needs refinement—perhaps "leadership presence" has been achieved and a new goal is needed. (4) If after 2-3 more sessions nothing shifts, explicitly change the coaching approach: different framework, different session structure, different type of between-session work.

### Answer 5: The Know-It-All

Surface compliance is a common challenge. The client is intellectually engaged but may be avoiding emotional vulnerability. They may be treating coaching as performance rather than exploration.

Approach: Acknowledge their expertise genuinely—they have done the reading and have frameworks. Then gently invite them beneath the intellectual surface: "You clearly know a lot about this. What would it look like to set aside what you know for a moment and just notice what's actually happening?" Use worksheets that require reflection rather than analysis (e.g., emotional awareness exercises rather than strategic planning). Consider naming the pattern directly: "I notice you often correct or redirect my questions. I'm curious what that's like for you." Build safety for vulnerability by modeling it yourself.

### Answer 6: The Client Who Wants Therapy

This is within coaching scope to acknowledge and hold space for—but may move beyond coaching scope if the client needs to process trauma. The fact that they are asking "is this something coaching can help with?" suggests self-awareness, which is positive.

Response: "Thank you for sharing that with me. That takes courage. Here's how I see it: coaching can absolutely support you in understanding how your early experiences shape your current patterns, and we can work on building the life you want. However, if there's deep processing of those experiences that needs to happen, that may be better supported by a therapist. Many of my clients work with a therapist alongside coaching. How would you feel about exploring both options?"

If the client continues to share detailed trauma or experiences significant distress in sessions, refer to therapy. Have your referral list ready.

### Answer 7: The Time Runaway

The short answer: you do not interrupt a breakthrough, but you also do not let the session run indefinitely. You compress and adapt.

What to say: "This is really powerful, and I want to honor it. We have about 8 minutes left. Let's take the last few minutes to land this—what is the most important insight you're taking from what just emerged? And let's set aside intentional time next session to build on this." This honors the breakthrough while respecting the session boundary. Then in the closing, offer a very brief acknowledgment and a simple commitment (the commitment can be "sit with this insight and notice what comes up").

If the client needs more time, offer an extra 5 minutes as a courtesy, but protect your next commitment. Breaks in session timing are acceptable occasionally but should not become a pattern.

### Answer 8: The Inconsistent Completer

50% completion is not acceptable if it is impacting session quality. The pattern needs to be addressed directly.

Approach: Name it with curiosity: "I've noticed that worksheets get completed about half the time, and when they're done our sessions are really productive. When they're not, it feels harder to build momentum. What's your experience of that?" Explore the 50%—is it random, or does it cluster around certain topics? Consider reducing the worksheet load to just what is most essential. Some clients do better with a single, powerful question between sessions rather than a full worksheet. The goal is to find the minimum effective dose of between-session work that keeps the coaching productive.

### Answer 9: The Contradiction

A contradiction that has persisted for three sessions needs exploration, not resolution. The client is likely aware of the contradiction and may feel shame about it, which is why they have not addressed it.

Approach: Do not lead with the AI summary. Lead with your own observation: "You've mentioned a few times that work-life balance is really important to you, and I also notice your weeks are very full with work. Help me understand how those two things live together for you right now." Stay curious, not confrontational. Explore the gap: What makes it hard to close? What would need to change? Is the stated priority genuinely theirs, or something they feel they should want? The contradiction itself may be the coaching material.

### Answer 10: The Reluctant Participant

This client is likely in precontemplation. They did not choose coaching and may not see a problem or a need for change.

Approach (first 2-3 sessions): Do not push for goals, action plans, or deep work. Build rapport and curiosity. Ask about their work, their perspective, their experience. Help them find their own reasons for engaging. "Your boss thought coaching would be helpful. What's your take on that? Is there anything in your work that you're curious about or would like to explore?" If they have no interest, name it directly: "It's totally okay if coaching doesn't feel right for you right now. My job is to help if it's useful. Let's just talk and see what emerges." Sometimes the most powerful intervention is giving the client permission to not engage, which paradoxically opens the door to engagement.

### Answer 11: The Session Notes Audit

The pattern suggests the coaching is drifting. No action items, no continuity, no shifts captured. The sessions may feel interesting in the moment but lack coherence and forward momentum.

Changes to make: (1) Re-contract with the client—review the coaching goals and ask if they still feel relevant. (2) Introduce a consistent session structure: always set a collaborative agenda in the opening, and always close with specific commitments. (3) At the start of each session, briefly reference the previous session: "Last time we talked about your relationship with your father. How has that landed?" (4) If the topics are genuinely unrelated, consider whether the coaching needs a more focused theme. A coaching relationship that covers everything often covers nothing.

### Answer 12: The Breakthrough

A breakthrough session requires a different kind of closing. The client has had an emotional and cognitive shift and needs time to integrate.

Closing approach: (1) Allow silence after the breakthrough—let the client sit with it. (2) Acknowledge the significance: "That's a really important realization. I want to acknowledge the courage it took to get there." (3) Do not pile on additional exploration or action items. The insight itself is the outcome. (4) For between-session work, do not assign a complex worksheet. The best assignment after a breakthrough is integration: "Between now and next session, just notice how this insight shows up in your daily life. You don't need to do anything with it—just observe." (5) At the next session, start by checking in on the breakthrough before moving to new material.

## Summary

Session mastery is tested not in ideal scenarios but in the messy, ambiguous, real-world moments that coaching presents. The ability to make sound judgments—when to push, when to pause, when to refer, when to follow the energy—is what separates skilled coaches from novices. Use these scenarios to sharpen your thinking before you encounter them with a real client.
