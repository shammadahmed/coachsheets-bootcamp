## What Is Appreciative Inquiry?

Appreciative Inquiry (AI) is a strengths-based approach to change that was developed by David Cooperrider and Suresh Srivastva at Case Western Reserve University in the 1980s. It emerged from a simple observation: **organizations (and people) grow in the direction of what they persistently ask questions about.**

If you ask "What is broken and how do we fix it?" you will find problems and generate problem-solving energy. If you ask "What is working well and how can we do more of it?" you will find strengths and generate generative, expansionary energy.

AI was originally developed for organizational development and large-scale change initiatives, but it translates powerfully to individual coaching. The approach is rooted in social constructionist theory — the idea that our reality is shaped by the conversations we have and the questions we ask.

**Core principles of AI:**
- **The Constructionist Principle:** What we believe to be real is created through our conversations.
- **The Simultaneity Principle:** Inquiry and change happen simultaneously. The moment you ask a question, change begins.
- **The Poetic Principle:** A client's life story is open to endless reinterpretation and re-authoring.
- **The Anticipatory Principle:** Our image of the future guides our present behavior.
- **The Positive Principle:** Positive questions generate positive energy and momentum.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Appreciative Inquiry is not "toxic positivity" — it does not ignore problems or pretend difficulties do not exist. It makes a deliberate choice about where to direct attention. Problems are acknowledged, but the coaching conversation is oriented toward strengths, possibilities, and what the client wants more of, not less of.

## The 4-D Cycle

The AI process is structured around four stages, often called the 4-D Cycle:

### 1. Discover (Appreciating)

The Discover phase is about identifying what is already working. The coach helps the client explore their "positive core" — the strengths, resources, capabilities, and peak experiences that already exist.

**Key questions for Discover:**
- "Tell me about a time when you felt most alive and engaged in your work/life. What made that possible?"
- "What do you value most about yourself? About your team? About your current situation?"
- "What are you most proud of?"
- "When you are at your best, what does that look like? What are you doing? Who is with you?"
- "What do others appreciate most about you?"

**Discover in practice:** This phase often involves storytelling. The client shares a "peak moment" and the coach listens for underlying strengths and values. The coach might say: "I noticed in that story that you were courageous, creative, and collaborative. Are those the qualities you want more of?"

### 2. Dream (Envisioning)

The Dream phase expands the client's vision of what is possible. It is intentionally aspirational — not constrained by current resources or obstacles. The coach helps the client build a vivid picture of their ideal future.

**Key questions for Dream:**
- "If you could design your ideal life/career/relationship from scratch, what would it look like?"
- "What is the boldest version of your future you can imagine?"
- "It is three years from now. Everything has gone as well as it possibly could. What has happened? What are you doing? How do you feel?"
- "What is your most audacious hope for this area of your life?"
- "What would you attempt if you knew you could not fail?"

**Dream in practice:** This phase can feel uncomfortable for clients who are used to practical, constrained thinking. The coach normalizes the discomfort. "I know this feels impractical. That is the point. We are building a vision first; the practical steps come later."

### 3. Design (Co-Constructing)

The Design phase bridges the dream and reality. The coach and client co-construct the "provocative proposition" — a statement that describes the desired future in concrete terms and proposes specific practices that will bring it to life.

The provocative proposition is written in present tense, as if the change has already happened:
- "I am a coach who prioritises deep listening over advice-giving, and my clients consistently report feeling heard and empowered."
- "Our team communicates with radical transparency, and weekly check-ins ensure everyone feels aligned."

**Key questions for Design:**
- "Given your dream, what specific practices and habits would need to be in place?"
- "What systems, relationships, or structures would support this vision?"
- "What is one bold step you could take to begin building this future?"
- "Who needs to be involved? What support do you need?"
- "What is the provocative proposition that captures this vision?"

### 4. Destiny (Sustaining)

The Destiny phase (sometimes called "Deploy" in newer AI literature) focuses on sustaining the change. It is about embedding the new practices into daily life and creating momentum.

**Key questions for Destiny:**
- "What is the first small step you will take in the next 48 hours?"
- "How will you maintain momentum when challenges arise?"
- "What accountability structure will support you?"
- "How will you celebrate progress along the way?"
- "What will you do when you inevitably face setbacks?"

**Destiny in practice:** AI recognizes that change is not a one-time event but an ongoing process of learning and adaptation. The coach helps the client build reflection and learning into their ongoing routine.

## AI vs. Deficit-Based Approaches

| **Dimension** | **Deficit-Based (Problem-Focused)** | **Appreciative Inquiry (Strengths-Based)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Core question** | "What is wrong and how do we fix it?" | "What is working and how do we amplify it?" |
| **Energy** | Problem-solving, often exhausting | Generative, often energizing |
| **Client experience** | "I am broken and need fixing" | "I have strengths I can build on" |
| **Coach role** | Analyst, diagnostician, fixer | Facilitator, amplifier, appreciator |
| **Outcome orientation** | Return to baseline | Move beyond baseline |
| **Risk** | Can reinforce deficit identity | Can ignore real problems |

The best coaches are not dogmatic about either approach. Some situations genuinely call for problem-solving. But AI offers a powerful counterbalance for clients who are stuck in deficit-focused narratives.

> [!WARNING]
> Avoid using AI with clients who are in crisis. If a client has just experienced a significant loss, trauma, or major setback, jumping to "what is working well?" can feel invalidating. In those situations, the client needs empathy, presence, and space to process — not active inquiry. AI is most effective when the client is stable but stuck.

## AI in Individual Coaching vs. Team Coaching

| **Context** | **AI Application** |
|---|---|
| **Individual coaching** | Discover peak experiences, dream about ideal career/life, design daily practices, build accountability |
| **Team coaching** | Discover team strengths and past successes, dream about ideal team culture, design team norms and rituals, embed practices |
| **Organizational change** | AI summits, large-group interviews, co-created strategic vision |

For a comprehensive guide to AI theory and practice, visit: [Positive Psychology — Appreciative Inquiry](https://positivepsychology.com/appreciative-inquiry/).

## Assignment

1. Identify a current area of challenge in your own life. Spend 15 minutes asking yourself only Discover and Dream questions (no problem analysis). Write down what you notice about the shift in your energy and perspective.
2. Draft a "provocative proposition" for one area of your life or practice. Write it in present tense as if the change has already happened. Read it aloud. How does it feel?
3. Practice a 20-minute AI-informed coaching session with a peer. Use all four stages. Afterward, ask your peer: "How was this different from a typical coaching conversation?"
