## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- Define life coaching and distinguish it from therapy, mentoring, and consulting.
- Explain the core premise of coaching: the client is the expert on their own life.
- Identify the three pillars of effective coaching: awareness, action, and accountability.

---

## What Life Coaching Actually Is

Life coaching is a structured partnership between a coach and a client designed to help the client clarify goals, overcome obstacles, and take sustained action toward a more fulfilling life. The coach does not fix, diagnose, or advise. The coach asks questions, listens deeply, reflects back patterns, and holds the client accountable to the commitments they make.

The foundational assumption of coaching is this: **the client is creative, resourceful, and whole.** This is not self-help platitudes — it is a operational stance. When you treat the client as the expert on their own life, you stop trying to be the expert *for* them. Your job shifts from "providing answers" to "asking the questions that help them find their own answers."

This is harder than it sounds. Most people who become coaches do so because they are good at helping people, which usually means they are good at giving advice. Coaching requires you to suppress that instinct and replace it with something more powerful: structured curiosity.

## Coaching vs. Therapy vs. Mentoring vs. Consulting

These four professions overlap in the public mind but have distinct boundaries. Confusing them is the single most common ethical trap for new coaches.

| | **Therapy** | **Coaching** | **Mentoring** | **Consulting** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | Healing past wounds | Future goals and action | Career/path guidance | Organizational problems |
| **Client** | Needs treatment | Functional, wants growth | Less experienced | Organization/client |
| **Expertise** | Therapist is the expert | Client is the expert | Mentor is the expert | Consultant is the expert |
| **Regulation** | Licensed, regulated | Unlicensed (ICF credential optional) | Unregulated | Unregulated |
| **Scope** | Diagnoses & treats pathology | Does not diagnose or treat | Shares personal experience | Delivers solutions |

The key distinction for coaches: **if a client needs healing from past trauma, clinical depression, anxiety disorders, addiction, or abuse, you refer them to a licensed therapist.** Coaching works with functional people who want to go from "fine" to "better," not from "broken" to "functional."

This is not a judgment about the client — it is a scope-of-practice boundary. A well-trained coach knows exactly where their competence ends and a therapist's begins.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The golden rule:** When in doubt, refer out. If a client mentions suicidal ideation, self-harm, clinical depression, trauma, addiction, or eating disorders, you are outside your scope of practice. Pause the coaching, express concern, and refer them to a licensed mental health professional.

## The Three Pillars of Coaching

### 1. Awareness
Clients come to coaching because they are stuck — but they usually don't know *why*. The coach's first job is to raise awareness: help the client see their own patterns, beliefs, values, and blind spots. This happens through powerful questions, reflective listening, and structured frameworks (like the Wheel of Life, values assessments, and thought records).

### 2. Action
Awareness without action is therapy-adjacent navel-gazing. Coaching is action-oriented. After the client sees the pattern, the coach helps them define concrete next steps — and commit to them. The action doesn't have to be big (sometimes the most powerful action is "sit with this for a week and journal about it"), but it must be specific, time-bound, and owned by the client.

### 3. Accountability
This is what separates coaching from conversation. The coach follows up. Did the client do what they committed to? If yes, what did they learn? If no, what got in the way? Accountability is not punishment — it is a structure that makes commitments real. Without it, coaching is just a series of pleasant conversations.

## Why People Hire Coaches

Understanding the client's motivation helps you position your practice:

1. **They have a goal they haven't achieved alone** (career change, habit change, work-life balance).
2. **They feel stuck and can't see the way forward** (values misalignment, imposter syndrome).
3. **They want a structured thinking partner** (executives, founders, creative professionals).
4. **They want accountability** (they know what to do but don't do it without external structure).
5. **They want a confidential, non-judgmental space** (they can't talk to family, friends, or colleagues about this).

Notice that none of these motivations require the coach to be an expert in the client's field. They require the coach to be an expert in *the coaching process*.
